Referral Tracking: What to Log When a Referral Comes In

By The Wellby team · November 5, 2026 · 4 min read

Key takeaways

  • Log a referral the day it arrives. Memory and month-end reports both let you down.
  • Record the facility and the person who sent it, as two separate fields.
  • Track the outcome and the reason for every loss, because that's where the lessons are.

  2. Which details should you log on every referral?

Good referral tracking starts the moment a referral lands, not at the end of the month when someone builds a report. If you capture the right few details at the door, nearly every question you ask later gets easy to answer. If you don't, no spreadsheet will rescue you.

What is referral tracking, really?

Referral tracking is a simple habit. Every time a patient or client is referred to your organization, you write down who sent it, when it came in, and what happened next. The value comes from doing it the same way every time, so the records can be compared.

It isn't the same as a CRM full of contact notes, and it isn't a monthly census report. A census tells you how many patients you served. Tracking tells you where they came from, and which sources are growing, shrinking or going quiet.

Which details should you log on every referral?

You don't need twenty fields. Teams that try to capture everything often end up capturing nothing, because the intake coordinator is busy and the form is long. Start with these seven:

  • The date the referral arrived, not the date someone typed it in.
  • The referring facility, whether that's a hospital, clinic, practice or residential facility.
  • The referring person: the physician, discharge planner or case manager who actually sent it.
  • The channel it came through, such as fax, portal, phone call or a message from a familiar contact.
  • The service it was for, whether that's home health, hospice or another line you offer.
  • The date you first responded.
  • The outcome: admitted, declined by you, declined by the patient, lost to another provider, or still pending.

Seven items, each one a pick-list or a date. A good intake coordinator can enter them in about a minute.

Why log the facility and the person separately?

This is the step most people skip. A hospital is a building, and referrals come from people inside it. When a strong discharge planner moves to another hospital, your volume from the first one can drop while the second one climbs. If you only log the facility name, you'll see the change but never the cause.

Logging both lets you answer questions like "which three people send us the most?" and "who used to send us patients and stopped?" Those are the people worth a coffee, a visit or a phone call.

Keep names consistent, too. "St. Mary's," "Saint Mary's Hospital" and "St Marys" will turn into three different sources in a report unless you pick one spelling and use a pick-list.

What should referral tracking record after the referral arrives?

A referral that arrives and doesn't convert isn't a failure to bury. It's a clue. Record the outcome, and when it's a loss, record the reason in a few words: no staff available, outside our service area, payer not accepted, patient chose someone else, couldn't reach the family. Coverage rules vary by payer and by state, so use categories that match how your own intake team really decides.

After a few weeks, patterns show up. Maybe one hospital's referrals keep dying on "couldn't reach the family," which points to missing phone numbers on their side. That's an easy, friendly thing to raise with their case management team.

Illustrative example

A home health agency logs 40 referrals in a month.

  • Hospital A sent 12 and 9 were admitted.
  • Hospital B sent 8 and 6 were admitted.
  • A physician group sent 6 and 5 were admitted.
  • Scattered smaller sources sent 14 and 7 were admitted.

That's 27 admissions from 40 referrals. Hospital A and Hospital B each converted 75% of what they sent. The smaller sources converted 50%, so half of those referrals went nowhere. The log doesn't say why, but it tells you exactly where to ask.

How do you keep the log honest week to week?

A log is only as good as the habit behind it. Three things help.

  • Make one person responsible for it, usually the intake lead, and give them a short weekly check: any referral still pending after a week, any blank fields, any source name that looks like a duplicate.
  • Enter referrals the day they arrive. By Friday memory has faded, and "I'll catch up Monday" turns into a pile of guesses.
  • Review it together, even for fifteen minutes. When marketers and intake staff look at the same page, disagreements get settled early, like whether a phone call from a physician's office counts as a referral if no paperwork followed.

Pick a rule for edge cases and write it down in one sentence. The exact rule matters less than everyone following the same one.

What do you do with the log once you have it?

Once you have a month or two of clean entries, sort by the person who referred and look at three things: who sends the most, who converts best, and who sent steadily before and has gone quiet. Those three lists are a visit plan for the next month, and a far better starting point for a conversation with a referral partner than a hunch.
