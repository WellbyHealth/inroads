Partner retention

How to spot a referral partner going quiet before you lose them

By the Wellby team · November 3, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Every source has its own normal rhythm. A gap only matters relative to that rhythm.
  • Catch the change early, while the relationship is still easy to repair.
  • Lead the win-back with something useful to them, not a request for more referrals.

Referral partners almost never call to say they've started sending patients elsewhere. It happens gradually: a new discharge planner who doesn't know you, a competitor who answered faster on a Friday, one bad experience nobody passed along. By the time the drop shows up in a monthly report, the new habit has already formed.

The fix is to watch each partner against its own rhythm, and act on the first gap rather than the third.

Every partner has its own rhythm

A hospital that sends you patients every few days and a physician who refers a few times a year can't be judged by the same rule. A two-week silence is alarming from the first and meaningless from the second. The question isn't "how long since their last referral?" but "is this unusual for them?"

Look often, act early

Monthly reports are usually too slow. The whole value is in noticing the change while it's still small and the relationship is easy to repair.

Rule out the boring explanations first

Before you assume you've lost the partner, check:

  • Did their volume drop across the board, because of a census dip, a unit closure or the holidays?
  • Did a key contact leave, go on leave or change roles?
  • Did anything go wrong on your side recently, such as a missed start of care, a slow response, or a staffing gap in their area?
  • Did a payer or network change make you a less obvious choice?

Your intake and operations teams often know the answer before the marketing team does. Ask them.

Run a win-back that's about them

"We noticed you haven't referred lately" puts the partner on the defensive. A better first touch gives them something useful:

  • A short update on outcomes for patients they've sent you, keeping it at the level of the relationship and leaving patient details out of routine email.
  • News that matters to them: a new service line, expanded hours, coverage in a neighborhood they serve.
  • An offer to solve a problem they have, such as faster acceptance for complex discharges or an in-service for new staff.

Then ask for a short visit. If there was a service problem, own it plainly and say what changed. Partners forgive mistakes much faster than silence.

Close the loop

Note what you tried and whether the partner came back. Over time, that record tells you which win-back approaches work with which kinds of partners.
