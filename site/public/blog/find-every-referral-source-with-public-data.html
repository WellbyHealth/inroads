Territory planning

How to find every referral source in your territory

By the Wellby team · November 3, 2026 · 4 min read

Key takeaways

  • Most referral lists are years old and miss the sources nobody has visited yet.
  • Public directories are a starting point, but the best sources are often local and personal.
  • Ask intake where referrals actually came from, and revisit the list regularly.

Most referral teams work from a list someone built years ago: the hospitals everyone knows, a few physician groups, and whoever the last marketer had a relationship with. The trouble is that the list quietly goes stale. Discharge planners change jobs, practices merge, and new facilities open down the road. If a source isn't on your list, nobody visits it, and nobody notices what you're missing.

Here's how to think about getting a complete, current picture of who could be sending you patients.

Think in drive time, not county lines

Referral patterns follow how far your clinicians and marketers can realistically travel, not the borders on a map. Start from each office you staff and think about the area it can actually serve. A suburban home health office and a rural hospice will draw very different shapes.

Know that public information exists

Federal programs publish a great deal about who provides care and where. The NPI Registry lists individual clinicians and healthcare organizations, and Medicare's Care Compare covers hospitals, nursing homes, home health agencies and hospices. These are useful for spotting names you've never heard of in your own backyard. Turning them into a clean, current list of the right people is real work, though: the raw data is large, inconsistent and changes constantly.

Remember the sources no database shows

Some of the strongest referral relationships never appear in any directory: senior centers, faith communities, elder law attorneys, geriatric care managers, assisted living communities and employer benefit programs, especially for private-pay home care. Ask your intake team where the last batch of referrals actually came from. Their answers often surprise people.

Sort the list into groups that need different plans

Once you can see the whole territory, most sources fall into one of three groups: partners who refer to you regularly, partners who used to and stopped, and sources who have never sent you anyone. Each group needs a different kind of attention, and treating them all the same is how teams end up busy without growing.

Keep it current

Facilities open and close, practices merge and people change jobs. A territory list you built once and never revisit slowly turns into a list of who mattered a few years ago.
