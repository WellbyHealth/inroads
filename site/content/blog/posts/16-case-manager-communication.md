Good case manager communication starts with respecting a crowded inbox. Picture one on a Thursday afternoon: discharge orders, family calls, payer questions, three messages marked urgent, and your update is one of forty. Here's the test: if they can read your message in ten seconds and know exactly what to do, it gets read. If it takes a paragraph to find the point, it waits until never.

## Why do case managers skip so many updates?

Not because they don't care. Most updates ask them to do work without saying what work. A long email that opens with "I hope this finds you well" and buries the question in line six reads as one more thing to deal with later.

Updates also fail when they arrive at random. If a case manager never knows when to expect something from you, they can't build a habit around it. Predictability matters nearly as much as brevity.

## What does good case manager communication look like?

Short, specific and built the same way every time. A simple pattern many teams adopt:

- **The answer first:** "We can accept Mr. R. for Saturday" or "We can't staff this one, sorry."
- **The one detail that matters:** the time, the missing document, the open question.
- **What happens next, and who does it:** "We'll confirm the visit time by noon. Please send the signed order."

Three lines. If a case manager only reads the first line, they still have what they need.

Skip the pleasantries in routine updates, and save the warm note for when something goes well or badly. A brief thank-you after a smooth discharge does more than ten generic sign-offs.

## When should you send updates, and through which channel?

Match the channel to the urgency. Something that affects tonight's discharge deserves a phone call or whatever your hospital partner prefers for time-sensitive items. A routine status can go through the portal or secure message. A weekly summary can wait for the end of the week.

Ask each case manager once how they like to be reached, and write it down. Some want texts for quick yes-or-no questions; others would rather you never text. Their preference beats yours.

Whatever the channel, patient information is sensitive. Use only the channels your organization and the hospital approve, share the minimum needed, and follow your own policies and counsel's guidance. When in doubt, call instead of writing details.

## How do you write the message they'll open?

Subject lines carry a lot of weight. "Referral for J. M., decision by 3 p.m." gets opened. "Following up" does not. Keep the key time or action in the first few words.

Use plain language, not internal shorthand. Say "we can start care Sunday morning" instead of "SOC pending staffing." Avoid attachments unless they're requested, and name them clearly when they are.

One message, one purpose. If you need an answer and also want to share a service update, send two messages so the question doesn't get lost.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A hospice team sends 20 status updates over four weeks in its old long email style and gets 6 replies or acknowledgments. The next four weeks it sends 20 updates in the three-line format.
>
> - Old style: 6 of 20 acknowledged.
> - New style: 13 of 20 acknowledged.
>
> The acknowledgment rate went from 30% to 65%. The topics and the case managers were roughly the same, so the format is the likeliest reason. Still, with only 40 messages it's a hint rather than proof, and the team should keep watching.

## How do you keep the relationship warm without adding noise?

Do the reliable things. Answer when you say you will. Close the loop after a handoff, with a short note on how the patient is settling in, when the case manager wants one and your policies allow it. Tell them early about changes on your side, like a new intake line or a staffing shortage, so they aren't surprised.

Once a quarter, ask a simple question: "Is there anything we could do to make your job easier?" Then actually do it. Someone who has a say in the process tends to read what you send.

Remember, too, that patients and families choose their providers. Good communication makes you easy to work with. It isn't a way to push anyone toward you.

## What should you track?

Keep a short note for each case manager: preferred channel, best time, how fast they usually answer, and any past hiccups. Review it before visits and calls. That's enough to make the next message better than the last.
