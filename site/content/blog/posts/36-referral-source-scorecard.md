A referral source scorecard is a single page that tells your team which relationships deserve attention this month. It's not a report for the board or a dashboard nobody opens. It's a working document you can read in ten minutes, argue about for twenty, and turn into a visit list before everyone goes back to their desks.

## What is a referral source scorecard for?

Its job is to replace gut feel with a shared picture. Without one, the loudest or most recent story wins the meeting: "I heard the hospital across town is unhappy" or "that practice has been great lately." Maybe true, maybe not. A scorecard puts the same facts in front of everyone so the conversation starts from the same place.

It also protects you from the opposite problem, ignoring a quiet source because nobody complained. Quiet is often the first sign of drift.

## What columns should a referral source scorecard have?

Resist the urge to track everything. Five or six columns are enough, and each should answer a clear question.

- **Source (person and organization).** Who sent it, as two fields.
- **Referrals this month.** The raw count.
- **Compared with the prior period.** Up, down or flat, over a stretch long enough to smooth out a single odd week.
- **Admitted or accepted.** How many of those referrals turned into care.
- **Response time.** How long it took you to first reply.
- **Last contact.** The date someone on your team last spoke with them.

If you know the source's total volume, add a share column. If you don't, leave it out rather than guess. Which fields you can fill in will depend on your service line and your data.

## How do you read the scorecard without overreacting?

Look for combinations, not single numbers.

A source with high volume and low conversion deserves a look at your process. Maybe their referrals arrive without orders. A source with low volume and high conversion may be a small practice that likes working with you and just doesn't have many patients to refer. A source whose volume fell while your last contact is months old is the textbook case for a friendly visit.

Be careful with tiny counts. A source that sends two referrals one month and none the next hasn't necessarily changed anything. Choose a minimum volume before you flag a trend, and write the rule on the page.

## How do you turn the scorecard into actions?

This is the step teams skip. After reading the page, pick no more than a handful of names and write one next step beside each. Examples:

- Call the case manager at the hospital whose referrals dropped and ask how things are going.
- Send the physician group a quick note about your updated service area.
- Thank the planner whose last three referrals went smoothly.
- Fix the response gap on the unit where replies were slowest.

Assign each to a person and a date. At the next review, the first item on the agenda is what happened with last month's list.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A hospice marketing team reviews four sources in one month.
>
> - Source A: 10 referrals, 8 admitted, replied same day, last contact 2 weeks ago.
> - Source B: 12 referrals, 5 admitted, replied in two days, last contact 6 weeks ago.
> - Source C: 3 referrals, 3 admitted, replied same day, last contact 8 weeks ago.
> - Source D: 0 referrals, last month 6, last contact 10 weeks ago.
>
> Source A converts 8 of 10 (80%). Source B converts 5 of 12 (about 42%) and replies slowly, so it needs a process check first. Source C is small but converts 3 of 3. Source D fell from 6 to 0 with no contact in 10 weeks, which makes it the first phone call.

## How often should you review it?

Monthly suits most teams. Weekly is too noisy and quarterly is too slow to catch a source drifting away. If you have a large number of sources, review the top group monthly and the long tail every quarter.

Keep the meeting short. Intake, marketing and the clinical lead each bring one thing: what they're seeing from the field. The scorecard supplies the numbers, and the people supply the context. A drop in referrals might be explained by a unit closing for renovation, something no spreadsheet will know.

## What should a scorecard never be used for?

It should never be used to rank patients, steer anyone's choice of provider, or reward people for referring. Patients and families choose their own providers, and the scorecard exists to help you serve sources better, not to pressure them. If any part of your outreach could be read as paying for or trading on referrals, stop and ask your compliance lead first. Privacy rules differ by organization and state, so keep patient details out of the scorecard and follow your own policies.
