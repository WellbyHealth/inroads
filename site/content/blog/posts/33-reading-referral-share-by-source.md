Referral share is the portion of a source's referrals that come to you instead of going somewhere else. It's one of the most useful numbers in business development, and also one of the easiest to misread. A rising count of referrals can hide a falling share, and a falling count can hide a rising one.

## What does referral share actually measure?

Say a hospital unit discharges patients who need home care every week. Some go to your [home health](/specialties#home-health) agency, some go to others, and some go home without services. Your referral share is your slice of the ones that go to an agency like yours.

The tricky part is that you rarely see the whole pie. You know how many referrals you got. You may not know how many the source made in total. Sometimes a source will tell you, sometimes you can estimate from a discharge report they share, and sometimes you have only your own count. Be honest with yourself about which of those you have, and label the number accordingly.

## Why can your count go up while your share goes down?

Imagine a busy winter. A clinic doubles its referrals to home health overall. You get more than last month, but a competitor got even more. Your count rose; your share fell. If you only watch counts, you'll celebrate right as a rival pulls ahead.

The reverse happens too. A source's overall volume dips because a physician went on leave, so your count drops, but your share of what remains is the same or better. Without the total, you'd panic about the wrong thing.

## How do you work out referral share when you only see your own side?

You have a few honest options, and each has limits.

- **Ask the source.** A case manager or practice manager can sometimes tell you roughly how many patients they referred out in a period. Treat it as an estimate.
- **Use shared reports.** Some facilities share discharge counts with their partners. Rules about what can be shared differ by organization and payer, so follow your own policies and counsel's guidance.
- **Track the split yourself.** If the source tells you at each referral whether other providers were offered, note that. Over time, you'll build a picture.

When none of these is available, don't invent a total. Track your own count and trend, and mark it clearly as "count only."

## When do small numbers fool you?

This is the trap that catches careful people. If a source sends you 3 referrals one month and 5 the next, that's a jump of two patients, not a trend. Share on tiny volumes bounces around for ordinary reasons: who was on shift, which unit had the discharges, a holiday week.

A useful habit is to set a floor. Decide that you won't react to a source's share until it has sent at least a certain number of referrals over a few months. The exact floor depends on your size. A rural agency and a metro hospice will set different ones. Write it down so the team applies the same rule every time.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A home care agency tracks two referral sources over a quarter. It knows each source's total because both share monthly discharge counts.
>
> - Clinic A made 40 referrals to agencies like this one. The agency received 14.
> - Hospital B made 120 referrals to agencies like this one. The agency received 24.
>
> Clinic A's share is 14 of 40, which is 35%. Hospital B's share is 24 of 120, which is 20%. Hospital B sends the agency more patients (24 against 14), yet Clinic A is the stronger relationship by share. The hospital is a bigger opportunity because 96 of its referrals go elsewhere.

## What should you compare share against?

A share number on its own is just a number. Give it company.

- **Its own history.** Is it higher or lower than the same source's share six months ago?
- **Your other sources.** Where do you win more often, and what do those relationships have in common?
- **Your capacity.** If you're turning down referrals because you're short on staff, a low share isn't a relationship problem.

That last point matters. A source may send fewer patients to you because you declined too many. The fix is operational, not another lunch visit.

## What should you do when share drops?

Treat it as a question, not an accusation. Pull the last several referrals from that source and look for patterns. Slow response? Payer you don't accept? A new contact who has never met you? Then reach out with something useful, such as updated service areas or a faster intake number.

And remember that patients and families choose their providers. Share helps you understand how well you're serving a source's needs. It should never be used to pressure anyone, steer a patient, or reward a referral.
