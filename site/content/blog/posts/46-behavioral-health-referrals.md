Behavioral health referrals run on quiet confidence. Picture a therapist whose client needs a higher level of care than she can offer. She isn't looking for the loudest program, she's looking for one she trusts, and that trust is earned in small ways.

## Who sends behavioral health referrals?

The mix depends on your program. Common sources include primary care physicians, therapists and psychiatrists in private practice, hospital emergency departments and inpatient units, schools, courts or probation (in some programs), employee assistance programs, and families who call directly.

Each has a different worry. A primary care physician wants to know the patient will actually be seen and that someone will tell them how it went. A therapist wants a good match and a warm handoff. A discharge team wants a clear next step with a date on it. A family wants to know their loved one will be treated with dignity.

## What do clinicians want to know before they refer?

Practical things, answered plainly:

- Who do you serve? Ages, diagnoses, levels of care, and any limits.
- Which insurance and funding sources do you accept? Coverage varies by payer and state, so be specific and keep it current.
- How long is the wait for an intake, and what happens in the meantime?
- Who will they speak to if they have a question?
- Will they get feedback after the referral, within what privacy rules allow?

A one-page description that answers these questions is worth more than a polished brochure. Update it whenever something changes.

## How should you handle privacy in behavioral health referrals?

Carefully. Behavioral health and substance use records often carry additional protections beyond ordinary health information, and the rules differ by program and state. Don't guess. Follow your own privacy policies and your counsel's guidance on what you can share with referral sources, what needs written consent, and what should never go into a casual email or an unsecured spreadsheet.

In your tracking, log only what you need to know. A source, a date, a service line and an outcome category are usually enough for relationship work. You rarely need diagnostic detail in your marketing data, and keeping it out lowers the risk.

## How do you build trust without pushing?

Show up reliably and be honest about fit. If a referral isn't right for your program, say so promptly and, where appropriate, suggest the kind of service that might be. A partner who hears "this isn't a match, and here's why" learns to send you better referrals later. A partner who hears nothing for a week learns to stop.

Respect patient choice. People seeking behavioral health care have the right to decide where to go, and a referral is a recommendation, not an assignment. Never offer anything of value in exchange for referrals. Rules on that are strict in many programs, so ask your counsel before designing any outreach that includes gifts, meals or events.

Be human in your follow-up. A short call after a clinician has sent several people is better than a mass email. Thank them for the trust, not just the volume.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small outpatient program logs 50 referral inquiries over a quarter.
>
> - Primary care offices: 20 inquiries, 14 intakes completed.
> - Private therapists: 15 inquiries, 6 intakes completed.
> - Schools: 10 inquiries, 4 intakes completed.
> - Families: 5 inquiries, 5 intakes completed.
>
> That's 29 intakes from 50 inquiries. Primary care offices completed 70% (14 of 20) and therapists only 40% (6 of 15). The gap might mean therapists are sending clients the program can't serve, or that their clients drop out before the first appointment. A phone call to the busiest therapist would help find out which.

## What should you track for each referral?

Keep it simple: the date, the source (facility and person), the service requested, how fast you responded, and the outcome. For outcomes that didn't end in an intake, a short reason helps: wrong level of care, outside our service area, funding didn't match, couldn't reach the person, the person chose another program.

Review those reasons quarterly with your team. If many referrals are declined for the same reason, the fix may be to tell your partners clearly who you serve, so fewer wasted referrals arrive.

## What does a good quarterly conversation look like?

Pick your top partners and ask them two questions: what's going well, and what makes it hard to refer to us? Listen. Write down what you hear and fix what you can.
