Skilled nursing referrals tend to arrive at the worst possible time: late on a Friday, with a family waiting and a hospital bed that needs to turn over. How you handle that moment says more about your facility than any brochure. The teams that do well with hospitals usually aren't the flashiest. They're the ones who answer.

## Why do skilled nursing referrals stall at the hospital door?

Mostly because of waiting. A referral sits in a queue until someone reviews the clinical notes, checks the payer, and confirms a bed. Every one of those steps can pause for hours, and the discharge team can't hold a patient's plan open forever. When they don't hear back, they move to the next option on the list, which is their job.

Other stalls are quieter: a missing document, an unclear payer, a family who hasn't picked a facility yet. Coverage and authorization rules vary by payer and by state, so ask your admissions team what each one actually requires and write it down in plain language.

## What do discharge teams need from a skilled nursing partner?

A few things come up again and again, in nearly every conversation with case managers and social workers.

- A named person to contact, with a direct line and a backup.
- A same-day answer, even when the answer is "we're reviewing and will confirm by 3."
- A clear yes or no on clinical fit, with a short reason when it's no.
- An honest picture of what you can handle: specific therapies, wound care, complex medication needs, language support.

A one-page capability sheet helps more than a thick packet. Keep it current. A sheet that promises something your unit can't do this month will cost you trust faster than a polite decline.

## How should you handle the no?

Learn to say it well. Declining a referral because you don't have the staff, the bed or the clinical fit isn't a failure. Taking someone you can't care for properly is worse for everyone. What matters is how fast and how clearly you say it. "We can't take this patient, here's why, and we'd be glad to look at the next one" keeps the door open. Silence closes it.

## Where do patient choice and fairness come in?

Everywhere. Hospital teams generally have to give patients and families a list of options and let them choose. Your role is to be a good option with accurate information, not to influence the choice or ask anyone to pick you. Never offer anything of value to a referral source in exchange for patients, and check your own compliance policies and counsel on what's allowed in meals, gifts and materials, since rules differ by state and payer.

If a family asks you questions directly, answer honestly. If they choose another facility, that's their decision, and the discharge team will remember how graciously you handled it.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A skilled nursing facility tracks one hospital's referrals for a month. There are 20 in all.
>
> - 14 were reviewed the same day they arrived, and 8 of those were admitted.
> - 6 were reviewed on a later day, and 1 of those was admitted.
> - Across all 20, the other outcomes were 6 declined by the facility and 5 where the family chose another facility.
>
> That's 9 admissions from 20 referrals. Same-day reviews led to admission for 8 of 14, about 57%. Later reviews led to admission for 1 of 6, about 17%. The sample is tiny and cases differ, but the gap suggests speed matters, and it's worth asking what held up the slower six.

## How do you build a steady relationship with a discharge team?

Start with the small, dependable things. Visit the case management office and ask how their process works from their side. Learn who covers weekends. Send a brief update on how a patient is doing once they've been admitted, when the hospital team wants those and your privacy policies allow it. Follow your own organization's rules and counsel's guidance on what you can share and how.

When something goes wrong, call before the problem grows. If a transfer was messy or a patient came back to the hospital, ask what the team saw and what you could have done differently. People remember that you asked.

## What should you track?

Keep it simple. For each hospital, log who sent the referral, when it arrived, when you answered, and the outcome. Add a short reason for every decline and every family who chose elsewhere. After a couple of months you'll see where speed, fit or paperwork is costing you, and you'll have specific things to talk about on your next visit.
