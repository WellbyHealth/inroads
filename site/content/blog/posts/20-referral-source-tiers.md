You can't visit everyone every week, and referral source tiers are a simple way to decide where your hours go. Somewhere on your list is a hospital unit that sends dozens of patients and a small clinic that sent two last year, and the choice between them should be deliberate rather than whoever called last.

## What are referral source tiers?

Tiers are groups. You sort every source into a small number of levels, usually three, and give each level a standard rhythm of contact. Tier one gets the most attention, tier three the least. The sorting rule is written down, so it doesn't depend on mood.

One thing to be clear about: tiers describe how you spend your time. They say nothing about how you treat patients or the people referred to you. Every referral gets the same prompt, careful response no matter where it came from.

## What should you sort on?

A few measures are enough. More than five and nobody will use the system.

- **Volume:** how many referrals the source sent over a recent stretch.
- **Fit:** how well those referrals matched what you can provide.
- **Conversion:** how many turned into admissions or starts of care.
- **Direction:** growing, steady or going quiet.
- **Potential:** the size of the source, even if they send little today.

Pick the measures that make sense for your type of organization, and use the same time window for everybody, such as the last six months. Mixed windows produce confusing results.

## How do you set the tiers?

Start with a plain list. Put your sources in order by referral count. Look for natural breaks, where the numbers drop off. Often a small handful sits at the top, a middle group sends a few each month, and a long tail sends rarely.

Then adjust with judgment. A new physician group that hasn't had time to send much but has a large panel may belong in tier two. A source that sent plenty last year but went silent may deserve a tier one visit, to find out what happened. A rule you can explain in a sentence beats a formula nobody trusts.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A home health agency has 18 referral sources and sorts them into three tiers.
>
> - Tier one: 3 sources, which sent 24 referrals in the period.
> - Tier two: 6 sources, which sent 12 referrals.
> - Tier three: 9 sources, which sent 6 referrals.
>
> That's 18 sources and 42 referrals. The 3 tier one sources sent 24 of the 42, about 57%. The 9 tier three sources together sent 6 of 42, about 14%. Half of the sources are in the bottom tier, so it makes sense to touch them lightly and spend the saved time on the top.

## What does each tier get?

Decide on a rhythm and put it on the calendar. For example:

- **Tier one:** a visit or call every one to two weeks, a named backup contact, and a quick review of any referral that went wrong.
- **Tier two:** a visit or meaningful message about once a month, plus a check-in after any change on their side.
- **Tier three:** a note or short call every quarter, a refresh of your contact information, and a response to anything they send.

Notice that what you offer is attention and information, not favors. Don't give gifts, payments or anything of value in exchange for referrals, and check your own compliance policies and counsel on what's allowed, since rules vary by state and program.

## How do you avoid ignoring the small ones?

This is the real risk of tiers. A tier three source is not a bad source. It may be a good partner that's small, or a promising one that's still getting to know you. Keep a low-effort rhythm in place so nobody falls off the map, and promote anyone whose numbers grow.

Also watch for a tier one source that starts to slip. Those are the relationships where an early conversation matters most. A drop in volume or a new contact at the top source deserves a prompt visit.

## How often should you re-sort?

Every quarter works for most teams. Rerun the numbers, compare against last quarter, and move sources up or down. It takes an hour or so if your log is clean, and it keeps your plan from going stale.

Share the tiers with intake and leadership. When everyone knows who is in tier one, a missed call from that office gets noticed quickly.
