Knowing how to follow up after a referral is a quiet skill that sets good teams apart. The referral came in, someone on your side did the work, and now the person who sent it is wondering what happened. A short, timely, useful message answers that question and shows you take their trust seriously.

## Why does follow-up matter at all?

Think about it from the sender's side. A discharge planner sends a referral on a busy afternoon and moves on to the next patient. If she never hears back, she doesn't know whether it was accepted, whether the family was reached, or whether she should send the next one to someone else. A little communication turns an act of faith into a relationship.

It also helps you. A quick note often surfaces a problem you can fix, like a phone number that was wrong or an order that's still missing.

## When should you follow up after a referral?

There are three natural moments, and each calls for a different message.

- **Right away.** A brief acknowledgment that you got it and what happens next.
- **When something changes.** Start of care scheduled, a delay, a need for more information, or a decline.
- **After a while.** A short thank-you or a check on how things went, once the case is well underway.

Skip the fourth moment, the one where you contact them just to ask for more referrals. That's the message that makes people stop answering.

## What should a good follow-up say?

Short, specific and useful. Lead with the news, not the greeting. Compare two openings:

"Hi, just following up to see if you have any more patients for us!"

"Hi, we've started care for your referral and the nurse visited yesterday. The family's doing fine and we'll update you if anything changes."

The second one gives the sender what she wanted. (Use only what your privacy policies allow, and if you're not sure how much detail is appropriate, ask your privacy officer or counsel. In some settings, a simple "care has started" is all that should go in writing.)

When a referral doesn't work out, say so plainly and kindly: "We weren't able to take this one because it's outside our service area. Here's who may be able to help." A fast, honest no is a service.

## How often is too often?

There's no universal number, because people differ. A busy hospitalist may want a two-line message and nothing else. A practice coordinator may like a monthly note. Pay attention to how someone responds and adjust. If a message goes unanswered more than a couple of times, step back and try a different channel before sending more.

Match tone, too. Some prefer a text-like brevity; others a proper paragraph. Notice how they write to you and mirror it.

## What shouldn't you do?

Never offer anything of value in exchange for referrals, and don't suggest that a patient be steered to you. Patients and families choose their providers. Your follow-up should inform the sender and care for the relationship, not nudge a decision. Rules on gifts, meals and gestures to referral sources vary by payer, state and the source's own policies, so check with your compliance lead before sending anything beyond words.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A hospice team sends follow-ups on 20 referrals from two sources over a month.
>
> - Source A received an update on 12 of 12 referrals, within a day of each change.
> - Source B received an update on 3 of 8 referrals.
> - Source A sent 12 referrals; source B sent 8.
>
> Updates were sent on 12 + 3 = 15 of the 20 referrals. Source A was fully informed (12 of 12), while Source B heard back on 3 of 8, so 5 of its referrals got no update. The team starts with those 5.

## How do you make follow-up a habit?

Build it into the work, not into willpower. When a referral arrives, create a small to-do for the next update. A template for each common message (received, started, declined, thank-you) cuts the time to a minute. A weekly check of referrals with no update sent catches the stragglers.

Keep a simple note of what you sent and when, so a colleague can pick up without repeating you. If you keep that in the same record as the referral itself, the history stays together.
