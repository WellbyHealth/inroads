Specialty practice referrals tend to follow quiet habits more than any brochure. Picture a primary care nurse with a patient still in the exam room, trying to decide which specialist to call. She isn't comparing credentials. She's thinking about who answers the phone, who can see the patient soon, and whose office sends back a note the doctor will actually read.

## What drives specialty practice referrals from one office?

Usually it's a stack of small things that feel like reliability. The referral form is simple. Someone calls the patient promptly. The visit happens when it should. The consult note arrives before the patient is back in the primary care office.

Doctors are also busy, so they remember what's easy to explain. If your group is the one for a certain condition, say so plainly. A one-page sheet that lists what you treat, what you need with a referral, and how fast you can usually see a new patient will do more than a long presentation. Only promise a timeframe you can keep.

Patient choice matters here as well. Patients often have preferences, plan networks or distance limits, and a good referral process respects them. Your goal is to be a trusted option, not to steer.

## How do you see which offices really send you patients?

Count by referring office and by referring provider, separately, ideally in one [source scorecard](/#scorecard). Offices have several providers, and the one who sends most might not be the one you've met.

Then look at the shape of the list. In many specialty groups, a few offices account for much of the volume, a middle group sends occasionally, and a long tail sends one patient and never again. Each group needs something different.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A specialty group logs 80 new referrals in a quarter from 25 referring offices.
>
> - 4 offices sent 48 referrals combined.
> - 11 offices sent 2 each, for 22 referrals.
> - 10 offices sent 1 each, for 10 referrals.
>
> That adds up to 80 referrals from 25 offices. The top 4 offices, which is 16% of the offices, account for 60% of the volume. The 10 offices that sent just one are only 12.5% of volume, but they're also 10 chances to find out what went wrong, or right, the first time.

## What should you do for the offices that send the most?

Protect the relationship. These are the offices you can't afford to lose to a slow callback. Ask what their staff finds hard about referring to you, then fix one thing at a time.

Send a short, regular update, nothing fancy: new providers, changes in hours, a new way to send records. Ask for a contact in the office who handles referrals day to day, because that person often matters as much as the physician.

Close the loop. When a patient has been seen, a brief note back to the referring provider tells them the referral landed. Share only what your privacy policies and counsel allow, and remember that payer and state rules differ on what can go in a referral reply.

## What about the offices that sent once?

Be curious, not pushy. Check the record first. Did the patient come in? Did the referral get held up because of missing information? Was there a long wait?

If the patient never made it to a first visit, that may be a scheduling or paperwork problem on your side that you can fix. If the patient was seen and the office didn't send again, perhaps they just didn't have another patient for that condition. A friendly note asking whether anything about the process could be easier is a good, low-pressure reach.

## How do you keep up with quiet offices?

Compare the last three months with the three before. Keep a list of offices that used to send and slowed down. Review it monthly. The goal isn't to chase every dip. Some practices have seasonal patterns, a doctor on leave, or a change in their own network.

A short, honest check-in works better than a pitch. "We haven't seen as many patients from your office lately. Is there anything we should be doing differently?" People respond to that.

## Does this need a big program?

No. Start with three habits: log every referral by office and provider, answer new referrals quickly, and send something useful back. Add one more habit when those feel natural.

Keep everything inside your compliance guardrails. Nothing of value should be offered in exchange for referrals, and every patient keeps the right to choose.
