Declined referrals are quiet data, and they're worth keeping. Nobody enjoys saying no, and the phone is ringing, the discharge is Friday afternoon, and you simply don't have a nurse for the weekend. You decline, the coordinator moves to the next call, and the reason lives only in her head. A month later someone asks why volume from a certain hospital dropped, and nobody can say.

## Why do declined referrals matter so much?

Because every one is a patient or family that asked for you and didn't get you. Some declines are right. You can't serve every address, every payer or every clinical need, and pretending otherwise is worse. But some declines are a staffing or process issue you could fix, and some are a misunderstanding on the referrer's side that a friendly conversation could clear up.

You can't tell which is which unless you wrote down why.

## What should you log when you decline?

Four things are enough:

- The date and time the referral arrived, and the date and time you declined.
- The referring facility and the person who sent it.
- One reason from a short pick-list.
- Whether you offered the referrer something helpful, like a suggestion that they try another provider, or a callback date if capacity was expected to open up.

The reason is the key. Free text becomes a mess. A pick-list keeps it countable. A sensible starting list is staffing or capacity, outside service area, payer or coverage, clinical fit, patient or family declined, and could not reach the family. Coverage and eligibility rules vary by payer, state and program, so write categories that reflect how your own team really decides.

## How do you sort the reasons into something useful?

Count them monthly and look at the biggest bucket first. Then ask whether it's something you control.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A home health agency declines 30 referrals in a month and logs a reason for each.
>
> - Staffing or capacity: 12
> - Outside service area: 8
> - Payer or coverage: 6
> - Clinical fit: 4
>
> That's 30 in all. Staffing is 12 of 30, or 40%, the biggest group. When the intake lead looks closer, 9 of those 12 came in after noon on a Thursday or on a Friday. That's 75% of the staffing declines, which points to a weekend coverage gap and not a general shortage.

That last step, looking one level deeper at the biggest bucket, is where the learning happens. Maybe the fix is a schedule change. Maybe it's an honest conversation with the referrer about what you can take on a Friday. Maybe nothing changes, but now it's a decision and not a drift.

## How should you talk to the referrer about a decline?

Quickly and kindly. The faster a hospital planner hears "we can't take this one," the faster she can call someone else, and the less likely the patient is to wait. Say what you can, not just what you can't: "We can't staff this one until Monday, but we can take it then if the family prefers to wait."

Never steer or pressure the patient, and never imply that choosing someone else will cause a problem. Patient choice always stands.

If you see a pattern, such as repeated out-of-area referrals from one office, share it gently. "We've had a few referrals from your team for the north side, and our service area stops at the river. Would a map help your discharge planners?" That tends to be welcome, because it saves their time.

## What about declines by the patient or family?

Log them too, with a reason when the family offers one. Maybe they picked a provider closer to home, or they chose to wait, or they decided not to go forward. You don't need to ask them to justify it, and you shouldn't push. Their reason, if shared, may reveal something useful, such as a wait that was too long or a call that came at a hard time. Keep notes brief and respectful, and follow your own privacy policies on what to record.

## How often should you review them?

Once a month is plenty for most teams. Keep it short: look at the totals, the top reason, and any source with several declines in a row. Pick one thing to try the next month, and check the result next time.

Keep a running list of "could we have said yes" cases. They're often small: a missing form, a late call-back, a staffing gap on one shift. Fixing even one can bring in referrals you were already getting asked for.
