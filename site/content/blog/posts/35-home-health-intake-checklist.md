A good home health intake checklist is less about forms and more about removing the guesswork that makes a referral sit. When the intake coordinator knows exactly what to look for, the referral gets a clear answer sooner, and the person who sent it learns that your agency is easy to work with.

## Why does a referral stall in intake?

Usually it isn't a hard clinical question. It's a missing phone number, an unclear order, no face-to-face documentation, or a coverage question nobody owns. The coordinator reads the packet, sees a gap, and sets it aside to ask later. Later becomes tomorrow, and by tomorrow the family has picked another agency or the discharge happened without you.

A checklist makes the gaps visible in the first minute instead of the fifth hour. It also turns "I'll get back to you" into "here's the one thing I need."

## What belongs on a home health intake checklist?

Keep it short enough to run while the packet is open. If you also serve [skilled nursing](/specialties#skilled-nursing) or [home care](/specialties#home-care) clients, you may want a lighter variant for them. Requirements differ by payer, program and state, so treat this as a starting skeleton and adjust it with your clinical and compliance leads.

**Who and where**
- Patient name, date of birth and a phone number for the patient or family
- Address, including access notes such as a gate code or a stairs-only entry
- Referring person, facility and a direct callback number

**What and why**
- The reason for the referral, in plain words
- The ordering clinician and whether orders are signed or still pending
- Diagnosis and recent hospital stay, if any
- Services requested (nursing, therapy, aide, others)

**Who pays**
- Payer and member information
- Any authorization or eligibility check your team needs to start
- Documentation your payer requires before start of care

**Can we do it**
- Is the address in your service area?
- Do you have a clinician available in the needed window?
- Any needs you can't meet, such as a language, equipment or visit frequency?

## How fast should the first answer be?

Faster than feels natural. The first response doesn't need to be a final decision. It can be, "We have the referral, we need X, and we'll confirm by Y." That gives the planner something to tell the family and lets her stop wondering.

Set an internal target and write it on the checklist, such as "first response same day." Pick a number your team can really meet, since a target missed every week teaches everyone to ignore it. Friday afternoons deserve a plan of their own: who's covering, and what happens to a referral that comes in at four-thirty.

## How do you say no in a way that helps?

Sometimes the answer is no. You may not serve the area, have the staff, or accept the payer. A quick, clear no is a service to the referral source, because they can move on to the next option while the patient is still in the building.

Give a one-line reason, in the same words every time. "Outside our service area" and "no clinician available this week" are different messages, and a source who hears the second may send the next patient anyway. A source who hears nothing may stop sending.

Log the reason in your referral record too. Over a quarter, those reasons add up to an honest picture of where you're losing patients for reasons within your control.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> An agency's intake team reviews 50 referrals from one month and groups the ones that were not accepted.
>
> - 31 were accepted.
> - 7 were declined for service area.
> - 5 were declined for staffing.
> - 4 stalled on missing orders and were never completed.
> - 3 went to another agency before the team replied.
>
> That's 31 + 7 + 5 + 4 + 3 = 50. The 19 that didn't convert include 4 stalled for missing orders and 3 lost to slow replies, so 7 of the 19 trace back to the agency's own process. That's where a better checklist would help first.

## How do you keep the checklist from becoming a burden?

Review it every few months with the people who use it. Remove any line nobody has needed. Add one when the same question keeps surfacing late. And keep a template message ready for the gaps you ask about most, for example a polite note to the referring office asking for a signed order, so that your coordinator isn't rewriting it five times a day.

Train backup coverage on it, too. The checklist is most valuable on the day your lead coordinator is out.
