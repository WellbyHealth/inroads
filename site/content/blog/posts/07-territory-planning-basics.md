Territory planning sounds like something for big sales organizations, but any team with two or more liaisons already has one, whether they've drawn it or not. The question is whether it was set on purpose or just happened when somebody said, "I'll take the north side."

## What does territory planning mean in a referral business?

It's the set of referral sources one person is responsible for. That might be a geographic area, a group of hospitals, a type of source (all the physician offices, say) or a service line, such as hospice or home health.

The point of a territory isn't control. It's clarity. When a source has a clear owner, someone notices when they go quiet, and the source isn't getting three different visits from three different people in one week.

## Should you divide by geography or by source type?

Both work, and many teams blend them. Each has trade-offs.

- **Geography** cuts down drive time and makes sense where distances are real. It can hide the fact that one area has twenty small offices and another has one big hospital.
- **Source type** lets someone become expert in, say, hospital discharge teams or skilled nursing communities. It can mean a lot of overlapping driving.
- **Service line** suits organizations offering quite different services, where the conversation changes completely between, for instance, [home care](/specialties#home-care) and hospice.

There's no single right answer. Choose the one that keeps your people on the road less and in useful conversations more.

## How do you make territories fair?

Equal square miles isn't fair. Equal numbers of sources isn't either, since one big hospital can take more effort than ten small offices. Think in terms of workload and opportunity:

1. How many active sources are in the territory, and how often do they need contact?
2. How much travel does a normal week involve?
3. How much current volume comes from the territory, and how much room is left?
4. How many sources are quiet or brand new and need extra effort?

You aren't after perfect equality. You want each person to have a job that's possible to do well.

## What does your data say about where to focus?

Compare two things: where your referral sources are, and where your admissions actually come from. A mismatch tells you where effort is going and where it's paying off.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A made-up agency has 40 referral sources split into three areas, and counts last quarter's admissions from each.
>
> - North: 18 sources, 9 admissions
> - Central: 12 sources, 20 admissions
> - South: 10 sources, 11 admissions
>
> The sources total 40 and the admissions total 40. North holds 45% of the sources but produced only 22.5% of the admissions, about half an admission per source. Central holds 30% of the sources and produced 50% of the admissions, around 1.7 per source. That doesn't mean North deserves less attention. It may be under-served, or it may be a poor fit. It does mean the team should find out which.

## How do you handle the awkward parts?

A few situations come up in nearly every plan.

- **Shared sources.** A large hospital might have discharge teams for several service lines. Name a lead contact for each relationship and agree who talks to whom.
- **Rep changes.** When a liaison leaves, the handoff matters more than the map. Write down what each source prefers and what's been promised before the person walks out.
- **Existing friendships.** People get attached to their sources. Transfers go better when you explain the reason and introduce the new owner in person.
- **Referral rules.** Which activities are acceptable with referral partners varies by payer, program and state, so keep each person's territory work inside your compliance policies.

## How often should you revisit the plan?

Quarterly is a reasonable pace, with extra reviews when something big changes: a hospital merges, a major contact moves, you open a new location or add a service. A territory that made sense a year ago may be lopsided now.

At each review, ask what you'd change if you were drawing the map from scratch today. Then change only what's worth the disruption.

## What's the simplest way to start?

Write down your sources, put an owner next to each, and compare the list against your admissions. That alone will show you more than most teams have seen. Reassign what's clearly unbalanced, leave the rest, and set a date to look again.
