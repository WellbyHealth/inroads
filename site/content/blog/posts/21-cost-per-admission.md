At some point an owner or board member asks the question: what does it cost us to get a new admission? Cost per admission is a fair thing to ask, and it's easy to calculate badly. The goal here is a number you can defend, with a clear idea of what it does and doesn't tell you.

## What is cost per admission?

It's a simple division. Take what you spent on outreach during a period, then divide it by the number of admissions you had in that same period. If you spent $6,000 and admitted 15 patients, the figure is $400 per admission.

That's the whole formula. The hard part is deciding what goes into each side, and being consistent from month to month.

## What costs should you include?

Be generous in what you count, or the number will flatter you. Think about everything that goes into your outreach effort:

- The pay and benefits of the people doing the outreach, or the share of their time spent on it.
- Mileage and vehicle costs for field visits.
- Printed materials, event fees and sponsorships.
- Software, subscriptions and phone costs related to outreach.
- Meals or materials for partners, only where your policies and the rules in your state allow.

Don't forget time. A liaison who spends half the week on outreach and half on other work should be counted at half, not zero and not full. Whatever split you choose, write it down and use it every time.

## Which admissions should you count?

Count admissions that came through your outreach and referral channels in the same period. Be careful about two traps. First, admissions that would have come regardless, such as a returning patient or a family who found you on their own, may not belong in an outreach calculation. Second, referrals take time. A visit in October may produce an admission in December, so a single month can look misleading.

Many teams smooth this out by using a rolling three-month window for both costs and admissions. It's less jumpy and closer to how referral relationships actually work.

If you serve different programs, such as [home health](/specialties#home-health) and another line, calculate them separately when you can. Costs and admission patterns often differ.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small agency adds up one month of outreach costs.
>
> - Liaison pay and benefits for outreach time: $4,500
> - Mileage: $350
> - Printed materials and events: $650
> - Software and phone: $500
>
> That's $6,000 in total. The agency admits 15 patients that month from referral sources. $6,000 divided by 15 is $400 per admission. If the next month has the same costs but only 10 admissions, the figure rises to $600, even though the team worked just as hard. That's why a rolling window tells the story better.

## How should you read the number?

Carefully. It's a rough guide, not a score. A rising figure might mean outreach is less effective, or that admissions dipped for reasons you don't control, such as staffing limits, a payer change or a seasonal pattern. A falling figure might mean the team is doing well, or that you pulled back on visits and the effect hasn't shown up yet.

Compare against your own history, not against other organizations. Your payer mix, geography and services make outside comparisons shaky, and rules vary by program and state.

Also keep in mind what the number leaves out. Admission is not the same as a good outcome, and a low cost per admission is not a reason to cut corners on care or on honesty with patients and families. Patient choice always stands.

## How can you break it down by source?

This is where the number becomes useful. If you log the source of every admission, you can see which relationships produce admissions and what time they take. Allocate costs by effort, such as the hours you spent with each partner, as long as you're consistent.

Be careful with small samples. A partner who sent two admissions this quarter isn't proven to be great or poor. Look at several months before you act, and let the patterns confirm what the numbers hint at.

## What should you do next?

Pick your window, write down your cost list, and calculate the first number. Then repeat it every month or quarter. After half a year you'll have a trend, and the trend is worth far more than any single figure.
