Your first visit to a hospital unit is short, and discharge planner questions are the best tool you have for making it count. A planner who's between a family meeting and a bed-management call doesn't need a brochure recited at her. She needs someone who understands her day and asks questions that show it.

## Why start with questions instead of your pitch?

Because the planner already knows what home health, hospice or home care is. What she doesn't know is whether you'll answer the phone on a Friday at four, or whether sending a patient your way will make her afternoon easier or harder.

Questions do two jobs. They tell you how that unit actually works, and they signal that you're there to fit into her process instead of redirecting it. People remember the visitor who listened.

Keep it to a few minutes. If she wants to keep talking, great. If she has to leave, you've still left a good impression.

## Which discharge planner questions explain how the unit works?

These are the questions that teach you the most:

- **"Walk me through what happens when a patient is ready to go home who needs services."** You'll hear who decides, who talks to the family, and where the paperwork goes.
- **"Who else is involved besides you?"** Physicians, nurses, social workers, case managers and sometimes the family's insurance contact all play roles. Knowing who can influence a referral helps you ask for the right introductions.
- **"What's the usual timing?"** Some units start planning at admission. Others plan the day before discharge. Your response speed should match.
- **"How do you like to send referrals?"** Portal, fax, phone, electronic record message. Use her way, not yours.

Policies differ by hospital, health system and payer, so don't assume the process you learned elsewhere applies here.

## What should you ask about problems she runs into?

This is where trust gets built. Ask something open-ended: "What's the hardest part of getting patients home safely?" Then stop talking.

Common answers are practical. Agencies that can't start care quickly. Referrals declined without explanation. Families who can't be reached. Equipment that arrives late. Whatever she tells you is a list of things you can fix or at least acknowledge.

You might follow up with, "Which situations make you wish you had another option?" If she mentions a patient type you serve well, mention it briefly and move on. Don't turn a question into a sales moment.

## How do you ask about patient choice?

Patient and family choice is part of every discharge. Many hospitals have specific rules about how choices are presented, and those rules vary by payer and state. Ask, "How do families usually get their list of provider options here?" and "Is there anything I should know about how you present choices?"

Then follow whatever she tells you. Don't ask her to favor you, and don't offer anything of value in return for referrals. You're there to be a good option when families are choosing, not to bypass the choice. If you're ever unsure whether a gesture crosses a line, ask your compliance team before you do it.

## What should you do right after the visit?

The visit itself is only half the work. Within a few hours, write down what you learned while it's fresh: her name and role, how she prefers to receive referrals, one problem she mentioned, and anything you promised. Keep the notes factual and follow your own privacy policies.

Then do the one thing you said you would do. If she mentioned that agencies are slow to confirm a start date, send her your intake number and your typical confirmation process within the week. A small, quick follow-through counts for more than a long email.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A marketer for a small home health agency visits 10 hospital units over a month and keeps simple notes.
>
> - She meets a planner on 8 of the 10 visits.
> - On 6 of those 8, she learns the preferred way to send referrals.
> - She sends a follow-up within a week to 5 of the 6.
>
> Of the 10 visits, 8 produced a planner conversation, 6 produced a usable preference, and 5 produced a timely follow-up. The gap between 6 and 5 is one planner who never got the promised phone number, a small leak worth fixing first.

## How often should you go back?

Not constantly. A good rhythm is a brief check-in after the first referral, a quick thank-you note when a case went well, and a drop-in when something changes on your side, like a new service area or additional staff. If a planner leaves or changes units, reach out to her replacement soon. Relationships live in people more than in buildings.
