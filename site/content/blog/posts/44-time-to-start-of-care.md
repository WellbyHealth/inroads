A hospital case manager has a patient leaving on Friday and three agencies on her list. She calls the one that picked up and said, "We can see him tomorrow." Time to start of care, the gap between a referral arriving and the first visit, is one of the quietest drivers of who gets the next referral.

## What is time to start of care?

It's the elapsed time from the moment a referral reaches you to the moment care begins. For a home health agency, that might be the first visit. For a hospice, the admission visit. For a skilled nursing facility, the arrival. The word "start" differs between services, so define it once and stay consistent.

On its own, one number hides too much. A referral that took three days might have waited two days in a fax queue and one day for a family to call back. Those are different problems with different fixes.

## Why does speed matter to referral partners?

Discharge planners work under pressure. They have a list of patients, a clock and a family asking questions. A provider who responds quickly and clearly makes their day easier. One who takes a long time to answer becomes a name they stop using when time is short.

This isn't about rushing care or cutting corners on assessments. Speed means no needless waiting: a referral that's seen the same hour it arrives, a quick call to confirm details, a scheduling answer that's honest. Your clinical standards, payer requirements and state rules still set the pace for what's safe and allowed.

Patient choice matters too. Fast shouldn't mean pushing. If a family wants to consider other providers, you let them, and you're ready when they decide.

## Which stages should you time?

Break the journey into four pieces and record a date and time for each:

1. Referral received.
2. First response, meaning a person acknowledged it to the sender or the family.
3. Accepted and scheduled, meaning you confirmed you could take the patient and set a time.
4. Care started.

Each gap tells a story. A long first gap usually means a queue problem: the fax sat, or the portal wasn't checked. A long second gap often points to missing paperwork, insurance questions or staff capacity. A long third gap might mean clinician availability or a family needing time.

Many teams are surprised that the longest delay is the earliest one, before anyone has touched the referral.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> An agency times 20 referrals over a month and records the average days at each gap.
>
> - Received to first response: 2.0 days
> - First response to accepted and scheduled: 1.0 day
> - Scheduled to care started: 1.5 days
>
> That's 4.5 days in total. Almost half of it (2.0 of 4.5, about 44%) is the wait for first response. Getting that first gap down to half a day would bring the total to 3.0 days without changing a single clinical step.

## How do you shorten the slow stages?

Start with the first gap, because it's usually cheapest. Decide who checks each channel, how often, and who covers lunch and weekends. Many agencies find a referral sat in a shared inbox that everyone assumed someone else was watching.

For the middle gap, build a short checklist of what you need to accept a referral and send it to your regular partners. When a hospital knows what to include, fewer calls go back and forth.

For the last gap, look at scheduling. Is there a standard slot held for new starts? Can a family be given two choices rather than an open question? Small changes often help.

Be careful with promises. If you tell a partner you can start care in a day and then can't, that damages trust more than a slower, honest answer would.

## How should you talk about speed with partners?

Don't boast. Mention it when it's useful. "We responded within the hour on the last three referrals" is a nice thing to say in a thank-you note, if it's true and you've measured it. A planner who's been burned elsewhere will notice.

Ask for their view as well. Where do they see delays on your side? Where on theirs? A missing phone number or an unsigned order may be the real culprit, and you can raise it kindly.

## What should you look at each month?

Pull the stage times and look at the slowest few referrals, not just the average. One outlier can show a hidden problem, such as a payer that always needs an extra step or a source whose paperwork is routinely incomplete.
