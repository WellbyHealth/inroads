You can win back a referral source, but only if you first work out why they left. A case manager who sent you four patients a month used to be the easiest part of your week, and now it's been two months without a single referral from her unit. It's tempting to panic, or to ignore it and hope. A calmer plan respects their time.

## How do you know a referral source has really gone quiet?

Compare. Take the last three months of referrals from that person and facility and set them next to the three months before. A drop to zero is obvious. A slow decline is easy to miss, so check your top sources every month.

Be careful about calling it too early. Referral volume rises and falls for ordinary reasons: a physician on leave, a seasonal pattern, a unit that changed how it works. One slow month isn't a reason to act. Several, from someone who used to be steady, is.

Also separate the person from the place. If one planner left and her replacement hasn't met you, that's a different situation from the same planner sending elsewhere.

## What should you check on your own side first?

Before you contact anyone, look inward. Did your response time slip? Did you decline several of their last referrals? Did a form change, or did a promised callback not happen? Did the contact person at your end change?

Pull their last ten referrals and read the outcomes. If three were declined and two sat unanswered for a day, you may already have your answer. Better to know that before you pick up the phone.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A hospice tracks a hospital unit that sent 24 referrals in the first six months of the year, then 6 in the next six months.
>
> - First six months: 24 referrals, or 4 a month.
> - Next six months: 6 referrals, or 1 a month.
>
> The drop is 18 referrals, which is 75% fewer. Reviewing the 6 months of lower volume, the team finds 3 referrals from the unit were declined for staffing in a single stretch of weeks, which might explain part of the change.

## What's the best first message to win back a referral source?

A short, honest question. Something like: "I noticed we haven't had the chance to help as many of your patients lately. Is there anything we should be doing better?" Say it in your own words, by phone or in person if you can, and don't write a long email.

Avoid guilt, avoid pressure, and never suggest that they owe you referrals. Patients choose, and referral sources decide based on what's best for the patient. Your job is to be a good option, not to demand loyalty.

If you did have a problem on your side, own it plainly. "We declined a few that I wish we'd handled differently, and we've changed how we staff weekends." Specifics are far more believable than apologies.

## What can you offer that's actually useful?

Offer help with the process, not gifts. Compliance rules vary by program and state, and anything of value tied to referrals is risky, so ask your compliance team what is allowed and keep it simple.

Useful, allowed things often include a one-page sheet on what you accept and how fast you respond, a direct line to a person on your intake team, a short in-service on a topic their staff asks about, or a clear way to see the status of a referral they've sent. These make the planner's life easier, which is what earns trust.

## How often should you follow up?

Gently and on a schedule. After the first conversation, set a small plan: one check-in a month for three months, each with something specific. A note about a change in your hours. An update that you added weekend admissions. A thank-you if they sent a referral.

Keep a record of each touch and what you heard. If the answer is that the person has a preferred provider, accept it and keep the relationship warm anyway. Sometimes a planner will come back simply because you were professional when they weren't ready.

## When should you let it go?

When you've listened, fixed what you could, and kept the door open, you've done your part. Some partners won't return, because their patients' needs shifted or their organization chose another path. Note it and put your time where it helps most.

Review the list of quiet sources once a month so they never sit unseen for half a year.
