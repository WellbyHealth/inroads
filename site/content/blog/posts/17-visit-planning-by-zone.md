Visit planning by zone is a plain fix for a familiar problem. Anyone who has driven across a county three times in one week knows it: the morning stop is north, the lunch meeting is south, and the office you promised to visit is back north again. The fix is simple: sort your partners by area, then visit one area at a time.

## What is visit planning by zone, exactly?

It means dividing your territory into a handful of zones and assigning each zone a regular slot on your calendar. Instead of building each day from whoever seems most urgent, you build it from where you'll already be. Tuesday morning is the east side, Thursday is the hospital district, and so on.

The zones don't need to be fancy. A map and a few colored pins will do. Draw lines around natural clusters: a medical campus, a retirement community corridor, the town thirty minutes out that needs its own half day.

## How do you draw the zones?

Start with where your referral partners actually are, not with a map of your service area. Pull a list of every source you work with or want to work with. Plot them. You'll probably see clumps without trying.

Then adjust for real conditions. A river with two bridges, a highway that jams at 8 a.m., or a one-way downtown can make two places that look close on a map feel far apart. Draw zones around travel time, not distance. Three to five zones is enough for most territories. More than that and the plan gets too complicated to follow.

## How do you decide who gets visited within a zone?

A zone gives you the route. It doesn't tell you who matters most. Rank the partners inside each zone using whatever your log can tell you: how many referrals they send, how well those convert, whether they've gone quiet, and whether they're a promising new contact.

Put one anchor stop in each zone visit, a partner who deserves your time, then fill the remaining slots with nearby ones. This way a short, high-value visit never gets sacrificed to a convenient but low-value stop.

Keep some flexibility. If a discharge planner calls with a question that needs a face-to-face conversation, go. The zones are a default, not a rule.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A liaison visits 20 offices a week. Before zones, she spreads them over 5 days and drives about 90 minutes a day. After grouping them into zones, she covers the same 20 visits in 4 days and drives about 60 minutes a day.
>
> - Before: 5 days x 90 minutes = 450 minutes of driving, which is 7.5 hours.
> - After: 4 days x 60 minutes = 240 minutes of driving, which is 4 hours.
>
> That saves 210 minutes, or 3.5 hours, plus a free day. She still sees 20 offices, but now has time for a longer conversation at the best ones or an extra visit to a source that's gone quiet.

## What about partners outside the zones?

Every territory has a few outliers: the clinic forty minutes from anything else, the facility that sends you only a few patients each year. Handle them differently. Combine them into a monthly "far afield" day, or use phone and video calls to keep the relationship alive between visits.

Be honest about whether a distant partner is worth the drive at all. If the answer is "not right now," a call and an occasional note may be plenty.

## How do you plan the day inside a zone?

Order stops to avoid backtracking, and check hours first. Some offices are only open to outside staff at certain times. Medical campuses often have a single check-in desk that makes the second and third stop faster, so put the stops that share one together.

Leave a cushion between visits for parking and waiting. Packing a day too tight is the quickest way to rush the very conversations you came for. Many liaisons plan one fewer stop than they think they can fit.

## When should you redraw the zones?

Every quarter is a fine rhythm. Look at where your referrals actually came from, which partners moved, which opened or closed, and where construction or new routes changed travel time. A new hospital or a large practice relocating may justify a whole new zone.

Also notice which zones you've been skipping. A zone that keeps losing its slot to other priorities is either less important than you thought or being starved of attention. Both are worth knowing.

## How do you know it's working?

Compare simple numbers over a few weeks: visits completed, hours driven, and conversations held. If visits go up and driving goes down, the plan is doing its job. If referrals from a zone start to grow, that's a bonus, though it takes time and many things influence it.
