Hospice referral tracking looks a lot like tracking for any other service, with a few quirks that can trip up a team borrowing a home health spreadsheet. The core idea is the same: know who sent each referral, what happened to it, and which sources are growing or fading. What changes is the timing, the mix of people who refer, and what counts as a good outcome.

## What stays the same in hospice referral tracking?

The foundation doesn't care about your service line. Every referral gets a date, a source facility, a source person, a channel and an outcome. Names come from a pick-list so "Mercy Hospital" doesn't turn into three different sources. Someone owns the log and checks it weekly.

If you already have that habit for another line, keep it. Don't build a separate system just because hospice feels different. One shared record means your team can compare sources across services, and that's often where the best conversations start.

## What is different about timing?

Hospice referrals frequently come in at the worst possible moment: a Friday afternoon call, a family at the bedside, a physician who finally had the conversation an hour ago. Speed matters in ways that a routine elective referral never does.

So log two times, not one. Record when the referral arrived and when a real person first responded to the sender. The gap between them tells you more about your relationship with a source than almost any other number. If a case manager sends you a referral and hears nothing for half a day, she'll remember it the next time.

Also note whether the referral came in during business hours. After-hours referrals tell you how well your on-call coverage is working, which is worth knowing even if it isn't glamorous.

## Who refers to hospice, and how should you log them?

The list is broader than you might expect. Physicians, hospitalists, oncologists, discharge planners, nursing facility staff, assisted living directors, home health nurses and families themselves can all start the conversation. Payer and program rules differ on who can formally certify or order, so use categories that match how your own team documents it.

Two fields help a lot here:

- **Referring organization and referring person**, separately, just like any other service.
- **Referral type**, such as clinician-initiated, facility-initiated or family-initiated.

Family-initiated referrals deserve extra care. They often point to a source you should be talking to, such as the physician who hadn't raised the subject, or a facility where families feel they have to ask. Handle privacy the way your own policies and counsel direct, and keep notes factual and short.

## How should you record outcomes that aren't a simple yes or no?

This is the biggest difference. In many services, a referral either gets admitted or it doesn't. In hospice, there's a large middle ground: the patient is eligible but the family isn't ready, the patient wants to keep pursuing treatment, or the timing is wrong by a week.

Give those their own outcome, something like "not ready yet." Don't lump them in with "declined." A family that says "not yet" today may call you in three weeks, and the source who made the introduction did a good job. If you log it as a loss, you'll under-credit that source and you'll forget to check back.

Other outcomes worth separating: admitted, patient or family chose another hospice, outside service area, not eligible under the payer's rules, and died before admission. That last one is hard to read and hard to log, but it matters, because it points to referrals that came too late, which is a conversation to have gently with the source.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small hospice logs 30 referrals in a month.
>
> - 14 were admitted.
> - 8 were "not ready yet."
> - 4 chose another provider.
> - 3 were outside the service area.
> - 1 died before admission.
>
> The outcomes add up to 30. Counting only admissions, the conversion is 14 of 30, which is about 47%. But 8 of the 30 are "not ready yet," so more than a quarter of the month's referrals are people the team can check back with. A log that called those losses would hide them.

## What should you review each month?

Keep the review short and practical. Sort by source person and look at three things.

- Who sent the most referrals, and how quickly did you respond?
- Which sources send a lot of "not ready yet" families, and do they know you're available for an early conversation?
- Who sent steadily last quarter and has gone quiet?

The second question is the quiet opportunity. If a source's referrals often arrive very late, an informational visit with their team about when a hospice conversation can begin may help everyone. Keep it about patient and family needs. Choice always stays with the patient and family, and nothing in your tracking should be used to steer anyone.
