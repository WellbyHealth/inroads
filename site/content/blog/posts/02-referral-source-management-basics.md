Referral source management sounds like a big program, but it starts with one honest list: who sends you patients, who used to, and who could. If you've ever walked into a Monday meeting and nobody could say when your team last spoke to a busy clinic, you already know why the list matters.

## What counts as a referral source?

A referral source is any person or place that sends patients or clients your way. That includes hospitals and their discharge teams, physician offices, nurse practitioners, rehab facilities, assisted living communities, and sometimes families who come back to you on their own.

Keep two levels in mind. There's the organization (a hospital, a clinic) and there's the person inside it (a case manager, an office manager, a physician). Most of the real relationship lives at the person level. The organization is just where you find them.

## How should you sort your sources?

You don't need a scoring formula. Three or four tiers, based on what's actually happened, will do.

- **Active:** sends regularly and you've heard from them recently.
- **Occasional:** sends now and then, with no pattern you can count on.
- **Quiet:** used to send, and hasn't lately.
- **Prospect:** has never sent, but could be a fit for your services.

Base the tiers on what you've logged, not on who you like talking to. Friendly conversations are nice, but they aren't the same as admissions. If you aren't recording referrals yet, start there, and the sorting will follow.

## Who gets your time?

Time is the thing you can't get more of. A common mistake is to spend it evenly, a visit here and a lunch there, with no plan behind it. Another is to spend all of it on the largest source and forget that one big relationship leaves you exposed if a key contact moves on.

A sensible split: most of your effort goes to active sources (keep them happy and informed), a steady share goes to quiet ones (find out what changed), and a smaller slice goes to a few well-chosen prospects. Where you draw those lines depends on your service area, your staffing and what you can actually accept.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small home care agency lists 30 referral sources and sorts them by last quarter's activity.
>
> - 6 active sources sent 36 referrals in total.
> - 9 occasional sources sent 9 referrals in total.
> - 15 quiet sources sent none.
>
> That's 45 referrals, and the 6 active sources account for 36 of them, which is 80%. Each active source averaged 6 referrals, while each occasional one averaged 1. The 15 quiet sources are half the list but contribute nothing right now, so the useful question is which of them used to send, and why they stopped.

## What does good referral source management look like week to week?

It looks boring, and that's the point. Every source on the list has an owner on your team, a note about the last contact, and a next step with a date. Nothing fancy. If a source has no next step, either it's healthy and you're doing nothing on purpose, or it's been forgotten. Make the difference visible.

A few habits keep it honest:

- Write down what you learned, not just that you visited. "Prefers a text before a call" is worth more than "dropped off brochures."
- Note who changed jobs. A planner who moves to another hospital is a new opening in two places.
- Record what you promised, such as a faster response on weekends, and check that you kept it.

## How do you stay clear of the line on referrals?

Patient choice always stands. Managing sources means being useful and easy to work with, not steering anyone. Rules about gifts, meals, marketing materials and what you may offer a referral partner vary by payer, program and state, and your own compliance policies come first. If you're unsure whether something is acceptable, ask your compliance lead or counsel before you do it, not after.

Being useful is a long list on its own: answer promptly, send clear status updates, tell them honestly when you can't take a case, and make their discharge day easier.

## How often should you review the list?

Monthly is a good rhythm for most teams, with a quick look every week at anything that's slipped. In the monthly review, ask four questions:

1. Which sources moved up a tier, and what did we do right?
2. Which moved down, and do we know why?
3. Who has no owner or no next step?
4. Which two or three prospects deserve real effort this month?

Keep it to thirty minutes. If the meeting runs long, the list is probably too big or too messy, and trimming it is the real work.

Over time, you'll see which sources trust you and which are still testing you. That's the information a good manager uses to decide where the next visit goes, whether you serve [home health](/specialties#home-health) patients or another line.
