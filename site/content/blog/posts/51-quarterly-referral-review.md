A quarterly referral review doesn't need a slide deck with forty charts. It needs a few honest pages that a busy executive can read in ten minutes and a team can act on the next day. Here is a four-page version you can build with whatever data you already have.

## What is a quarterly referral review for?

It answers three questions. What happened this quarter? Why do we think it happened? What are we going to do about it? Monthly check-ins catch problems early, but a quarter is long enough for patterns to show and short enough to change course.

It also makes a good habit for the team. Knowing a review is coming encourages cleaner logging and more thoughtful outreach all quarter long.

## Page one: how many referrals came in?

Show referrals received, admitted and not admitted, for this quarter and the previous one, month by month. Add a plain sentence below, such as "February was slow after the storm closed two clinics for a week." If a number surprised you, say so.

Keep definitions steady. If you changed how you count, say that on the page, or comparisons will mislead. Seasonal patterns and local events can shape volume, so don't read too much into a single quarter.

## Page two: which sources changed?

List your top sources with this quarter's count, last quarter's count and the difference. Add a short list of sources that sent nothing this quarter after sending before, and a list of new sources.

This is the page that drives conversations. A hospital that fell is a question for the liaison. A new clinic that started sending is a thank-you waiting to be written. A quiet source might have a changed staffing situation you haven't heard about.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small home care agency lists its top three sources.
>
> - Hospital A: 20 referrals last quarter, 26 this quarter.
> - Physician group B: 12 last quarter, 9 this quarter.
> - Clinic C: 5 last quarter, 0 this quarter.
>
> Together these sent 37 last quarter (20 + 12 + 5) and 35 this quarter (26 + 9 + 0), a drop of 2. Hospital A grew by 6, but group B fell by 3 and Clinic C fell by 5. The overall total hides that the real news is two shrinking relationships.

## Page three: where did referrals go after they arrived?

Show conversion: of the referrals received, how many were admitted, and where did the rest go? Use a short reason list: no capacity, outside service area, payer not accepted, patient chose another provider, could not reach family, still pending.

Also show time to first response, if you track it. Slow responses are one of the most fixable causes of lost referrals. Coverage, eligibility and capacity rules vary by payer, state and program, so keep your reasons in terms that match your own intake decisions.

Break conversion out by source when you can. A source with low conversion might need clearer information about what you accept.

## Page four: what are we going to do?

This is the page people read first, so write it last and keep it short. List three to five actions, each with one owner and one date. Examples: visit Clinic C and ask what changed. Send Hospital A's case managers a thank-you and a one-page summary of our new capabilities. Fix the first-response gap on weekends.

Resist the temptation to list everything. A review with twelve priorities has none.

## How do you present it without turning it into a debate about the numbers?

Agree on definitions before the meeting. Send the pages a day ahead. Start by asking what stands out, rather than walking through each chart.

Be honest about uncertainty. If you don't know why a source fell, say "we don't know yet, here's how we'll find out." Executives respect that more than a confident guess that turns out wrong.

Keep patient information out of the review. Aggregate counts and source names are generally enough. Follow your own privacy policies and your counsel's guidance on what belongs in a leadership packet.

## How do you make the next review easier?

At the end of the quarter, note what you wished you had. Maybe it was reasons for lost referrals, or consistent source names. Fix that in the first week of the next quarter, while the pain is fresh. And keep a copy of each review so you can see how the story develops over a year.
