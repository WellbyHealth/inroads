A wound that won't heal touches many hands. A primary care physician sees it first, a home nurse dresses it, a podiatrist weighs in, and eventually someone says, "This needs a specialist." Wound care referrals come from all those places, and the hard part isn't being open to them. It's finding the ones that matter and making it easy to send patients your way.

## Who sends wound care referrals?

Common sources include primary care and internal medicine offices, podiatrists, vascular and surgical practices, endocrinology and diabetes clinics, home health agencies, skilled nursing facilities, hospital discharge teams, and sometimes dialysis centers. The mix varies by community, and part of your first job is learning which of these actually send patients to a wound program like yours.

Notice that several of those sources are other care organizations that see wounds daily. A home health nurse who spots a stalled wound may want a place to send the patient. A skilled nursing facility may want a consult. Those relationships can be two-way.

## How do you find the clinics that might send?

Start with what you already know. Look at your own records: who referred in the past year, and who referred once and never again? Then look at who sends your patients to other places. Ask your current partners where else they see wound problems. Ask your staff who they already know.

Make a short list of target clinics. For each, note who the likely referrer is (often a nurse or medical assistant, not the physician), which services they offer, and what kinds of wounds they commonly see. Don't guess at numbers. Visit and ask.

## What do referring clinics want to know?

Plain, specific things:

- What kinds of wounds do you treat, and what do you not treat?
- How quickly can a patient be seen?
- What should the referral include?
- How will we hear back about what you found and what the plan is?
- What does a patient need to bring or arrange, such as transportation?

Coverage rules, authorization requirements and who can order what differ by payer and by state. Be honest about which apply to you, and don't promise specifics you can't guarantee.

A one-page referral guide with a direct phone number does a lot of work. So does a reliable report back to the sender, within what privacy rules permit. Follow your own policies and your counsel's guidance on what to include.

## Why do some wound care referrals never turn into visits?

Usually for ordinary reasons: the patient couldn't get a ride, the family wasn't told the appointment, the paperwork was incomplete, or the first appointment was too far away. Tracking these gaps is more valuable than counting referrals alone.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small wound care clinic reviews 40 referrals from one quarter.
>
> - Primary care offices: 16 referrals, 12 first visits.
> - Home health agencies: 10 referrals, 9 first visits.
> - Podiatry and vascular practices: 8 referrals, 5 first visits.
> - Skilled nursing facilities: 6 referrals, 2 first visits.
>
> That's 40 referrals and 28 first visits (12 + 9 + 5 + 2). Home health converted 90% (9 of 10) while skilled nursing converted one third (2 of 6). The clinic might ask the nursing facilities whether transport or paperwork is the obstacle.

## How should you build relationships with these clinics?

Visit with a purpose. Bring the referral guide, ask what happens to their patients with chronic wounds today, and listen. Send a thank-you note after a first referral that mentions something specific about the process.

Offer education only where it's truly useful and within the rules that apply to you. A short conversation about how to describe a wound in a referral can help everyone, and it isn't a pitch.

Patient choice stands. Some patients prefer a clinic near home or one their physician already uses. Never offer anything of value in return for referrals, and ask your counsel about the limits in your programs.

## What should you track?

Keep a simple record: date, referring facility, referring person, wound category (a short pick-list), how quickly the patient was scheduled, whether the first visit happened, and a reason if it didn't. Review monthly for sources that send steadily, sources with poor follow-through, and sources that stopped.
