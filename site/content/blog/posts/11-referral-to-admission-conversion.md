Referral to admission conversion is the share of referrals you receive that end with a patient actually admitted. It's easy to calculate and surprisingly hard to act on, because a single number doesn't say where the referrals went. The useful work is breaking the path into stages and finding the one that leaks.

## What is referral to admission conversion?

Take the referrals that arrived in a period and count how many became admissions. Divide the second by the first. That's your conversion.

On its own it's a blunt tool. A low figure could mean slow response, a service area mismatch, referrals arriving without enough information, or families choosing someone else. A decent figure can hide a particular source whose referrals mostly fail. The number is a starting point for questions.

## Which stages should you measure?

Four stages fit most teams, though yours may differ.

1. **Received.** The referral arrived.
2. **Responded.** Someone contacted the referring office or family.
3. **Accepted.** You reviewed it and agreed you could serve the patient.
4. **Admitted.** Care actually started.

Count how many referrals reach each stage, then look at the drop between neighbors. Eligibility rules, coverage and what you can accept differ by payer, program and state, so define "accepted" in a way that matches how your team really decides.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A made-up team received 100 referrals last quarter.
>
> - Received: 100
> - Responded: 90
> - Accepted: 70
> - Admitted: 60
>
> The drops are 10 (received to responded), 20 (responded to accepted) and 10 (accepted to admitted), which total 40 lost. Overall conversion is 60 of 100, or 60%. Stage by stage, 90% were answered, 78% of those answered were accepted (70 of 90, rounded) and about 86% of those accepted were admitted (60 of 70, rounded). The largest single drop is between responding and accepting, so that's where to look first.

## Where do referrals usually drop?

Each stage tends to leak for its own reasons.

- **Before a response:** unowned queues, after-hours arrivals, referrals that look incomplete and get set aside.
- **Between response and acceptance:** missing information, a service area or staffing limit, a payer you can't work with, or a referring office that never sends the remaining paperwork.
- **Between acceptance and admission:** the patient or family chooses another provider, can't be reached, isn't ready, or the start date slips.

Some of those losses are yours to fix. Others belong to the referring side or to the patient's own choice, and patient choice always stands. Part of the skill is telling them apart.

## Why record the reason for every loss?

Because counts say where, and reasons say why. Use a short pick-list so entering it takes seconds: no staff available, outside service area, coverage not accepted, information never received, patient chose another provider, couldn't reach family, patient not ready. Add "other" with a few words.

After a month, group the losses by reason. If "couldn't reach family" dominates a single hospital's referrals, a friendly call to its case management team about contact numbers may help. If "no staff available" dominates one zip code, that's an operations conversation, not a marketing one.

## How do you look at conversion by source?

Break the overall figure down by referring facility and person. Two sources with the same volume can behave very differently. One sends complete referrals for patients who fit your services. The other sends a stream of cases you can't take. Conversion by source shows which relationships are worth more of your time, and which need a conversation about what makes a good referral for you.

Be careful with small numbers. A source with three referrals and one admission isn't a pattern. Wait for more data before drawing conclusions, and use your judgment alongside the math.

## What do you do with what you find?

Pick one leak and try one change. If responses are slow on Fridays, name a weekend owner. If acceptance drops because of missing paperwork, send referring offices a simple checklist of what you need. Then watch the same stage for a month or two and see whether it moves.

Resist fixing everything at once. When you change five things, you can't tell which one worked.

## Does conversion tell the whole story?

No. It says nothing about whether a patient was well served, or whether the right call was to decline. A team that never says no may have a high conversion rate and a stretched staff. Treat it as one measure among several, alongside response time and the quality of your handoffs.
