Territory overlap is rarely a dramatic fight. It's two marketers who both visited the same clinic in the same week, a hospital unit that nobody visited because each assumed the other had it, and a physician who got two different answers about your services. The fix isn't a sharper map. It's a clear owner for every source and a simple rule for the exceptions.

## Why does territory overlap happen?

Because geography is a poor match for how referrals work. A zip code tells you where a building is. It doesn't tell you that a discharge planner at one hospital also sends patients from three counties, or that a physician practices at two locations on opposite sides of a boundary.

Overlap also grows when the team does. A new service line, a new liaison, a merger, or someone going on leave can leave two people covering the same source and nobody covering another. If assignments live in people's heads, they drift.

## What should you assign: places or people?

Assign people, specifically the individual on the referral source's side, to a named marketer on yours. Buildings are too coarse. A large hospital might have a dozen decision makers across different units, and a single marketer visiting "the hospital" will miss most of them.

A workable approach is to list sources at the person level, then group by organization. Each person gets one owner. An organization with many people may have a lead owner, with other marketers owning specific units or services. That way, the question "who talks to Dr. Lee?" has one answer.

If your team covers multiple lines, such as [home health](/specialties#home-health) and [hospice](/specialties#hospice), decide whether ownership follows the person or the service. Either can work, as long as it's explicit.

## How do you settle shared sources fairly?

Some sources will genuinely belong to more than one person. Don't leave them undefined. Pick a rule and write it in a sentence. A few options:

- **First relationship.** Whoever has the longer history with the source keeps it.
- **Service line.** One marketer owns hospice conversations, another owns home health, even at the same office.
- **Volume tier.** Top sources get a lead owner plus a backup; smaller ones get one owner.
- **Source's preference.** If a case manager prefers to work with one person, honor it.

Whichever rule you choose, apply it the same way each time. Fairness matters more than cleverness, especially if compensation or goals touch these assignments. Handle that part with your leadership and HR; this guide doesn't cover it.

## What should happen when two marketers both visit?

Sometimes it'll happen anyway. Prevent the awkward version by keeping a shared log that shows the last contact date and who made it. Before any visit, take ten seconds to check it. If a colleague was there last week, reach out to her first or adjust your visit.

When a source is shared, agree on who speaks for the organization on specific topics. A mixed message about service area or payer acceptance does more harm than a missed visit.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A home care agency has two marketers and 24 referral sources. After a review, the team finds this.
>
> - 9 sources were visited by both marketers within the same month.
> - 5 sources were visited by neither in the last two months.
> - The other 10 had one clear visitor.
>
> The groups add up to 9 + 5 + 10 = 24. So 14 of the 24 sources, more than half, were either double covered or not covered at all. The team assigns one owner to each of the 14 and a backup to the top three.

## How do you handle a marketer who leaves or changes roles?

This is where overlap and gaps appear. When someone moves on, assign her sources within the first week, not the first month. Ask her, or her manager, for a short handoff note on each important person: preferences, history and any promise outstanding. Introduce the new owner personally when possible.

In the meantime, make sure that the sources with the most recent activity get an interim contact. A source that hears nothing for six weeks after its liaison leaves will often assume you've lost interest.

## How often should you revisit assignments?

Every quarter is a good rhythm. Look at who is quiet, who has changed jobs, and whether any marketer carries too many sources to cover well. A person can only keep so many relationships warm, and the right number depends on how often your sources need contact. Rebalance when someone is overloaded, but try not to reshuffle everything at once. Moving relationships around too often is itself a source of confusion.

Keep the focus on service. Referral partners aren't prizes to be won by a marketer, and patients choose their providers. The point of ownership is to make sure someone is always paying attention, not to compete for credit.
