Good marketer onboarding doesn't start with a territory map and a quota. It starts with making sure your new liaison can answer the questions referral sources will ask on day one. If she can't explain how intake works, who covers weekends or what you can't accept, her first visits will do more harm than good.

## What should a new liaison learn in the first week?

Stay inside your own walls for the first few days. That feels slow, but it pays off.

Have your new marketer spend time with intake, clinical leads and the on-call team. She should sit in on a few intake calls, learn how a referral moves from arrival to start of care, and hear in plain terms why referrals get declined. If your agency can't take a certain payer or service area, she needs to know before she promises anything.

Give her access to the referral log and ask her to read the last few months. She'll start spotting names that repeat, and she'll have real questions for the first conversations.

## How should she inherit relationships?

If there was a previous liaison, do not just hand over a spreadsheet. A list of names tells her nothing about who likes a phone call, who wants a short email, and who was disappointed by something last spring.

Ask the previous marketer, or whoever covered the territory, for a short conversation about each of the top sources: what the person is like, what's gone well, what's a sore spot. If the previous liaison has left, ask intake and clinical staff who they enjoy working with.

Introduce her, if you can, instead of letting her cold-call. A line from a trusted colleague, such as "she's taking over the territory and she's good," saves weeks of awkwardness.

## What does marketer onboarding look like in weeks two and three?

By now she should be ready to meet sources, starting with the top few, not the whole list. A good approach is to visit people you already work with first. Existing partners are forgiving, they'll tell her what's working, and she'll learn the vocabulary before she meets people who know nothing about you.

Teach her to write a note within a day of every visit: who she met, how they prefer to send referrals, one problem they mentioned, and what she promised. Remind her to keep notes factual and respect your organization's privacy policies.

She should also shadow a senior colleague on at least a couple of visits. Watching someone ask good questions teaches more than any script.

## How do you teach the compliance basics?

Early, and in plain language. A new liaison should know that patients choose their providers, that nothing of value is traded for referrals, and that rules on gifts, meals and marketing to facilities differ by payer, state and by the source's own policies. Have your compliance lead walk through examples of what's fine and what needs a conversation first. Make sure she knows it's always acceptable to ask before doing something.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A home care agency sets a 30-day plan for a new liaison and checks it at the end.
>
> - Week 1: 0 outside visits, 6 hours with intake and clinical staff.
> - Week 2: 4 visits to existing partners.
> - Week 3: 5 visits, 2 with a senior colleague.
> - Week 4: 6 visits, including 2 new sources.
>
> The plan adds up to 4 + 5 + 6 = 15 outside visits in the month. Only 2 of those 15 were to new sources, and 2 were shadowing a senior colleague, so the month leaned toward learning over reaching, which is sensible for a first month.

## What should be in place by day thirty?

Don't measure the first month by referral volume. It's too early, and the number can mislead. Measure whether she's built the foundation:

- A short list of top sources, each with a named person and a next step.
- A habit of logging every contact and every referral she touches.
- At least a few sources who know her name and know how to reach her.
- A clear understanding of where she can say yes, where she has to check, and where the answer is no.

Hold a 30-minute review with her manager at the end. Ask her what surprised her, what she wishes she'd known sooner, and what she wants to do differently next month. Her answers will improve your onboarding for the next hire.
