Good intake coordinator scripts won't make a hard call easy, but they keep you steady. It's late Friday and the phone rings. A discharge planner needs an answer now, the family is standing beside her, and you aren't sure you can staff the visit until Monday. Whatever you say next matters to the patient and to the planner's opinion of your agency.

## Why use intake coordinator scripts at all?

Scripts get a bad name because people picture a robotic reading. That's not the idea. A script is a short skeleton you can lean on when you're tired, rushed or caught off guard. It makes sure you include the three things the other person needs: a clear answer, a reason in plain words, and what happens next.

Treat the scripts below as drafts. Change them until they sound like you. Check them against your own policies, and remember that rules about what you can say or share vary by payer, state and program. Never include more patient detail on a voicemail or in a message than your privacy policy allows.

## What do you say when you have to decline?

The aim is to be quick, honest and useful, so the other person can find care without delay.

*"Thanks for sending this over. I want to be straight with you: we can't take this one right now because we don't have staff to cover it on the days you need. I'd rather tell you now so the family isn't waiting. If it helps, I can check again Monday morning and call you if something opens up. Is there anything else you need from us today?"*

Notice what it does. It gives the answer in the first sentence after thanks, offers a reason, avoids blaming anyone and names a next step. It doesn't tell the planner where to send the patient, because the patient and family choose.

## What do you say when information is missing?

Most referral delays are paperwork. Ask for exactly what you need, once.

*"We've got the referral, thank you. To get started I'm missing three things: the face-to-face date, the signed order and a working phone number for the family. If you can send those, I can have someone call the family today. Who's the best person to reach if we have a question?"*

Say the list out loud and keep it short. If you can, put the same list in a message so nobody has to remember it. What counts as a complete referral depends on the payer and the program, so use your own checklist.

## What do you say to a worried family?

Slow down. Families on these calls are often tired and scared, and they hear tone more than words.

*"I'm glad you called. I'll tell you what happens next, and you can stop me with questions. Someone from our team will contact you [by a time you can keep] to set up the first visit. If you don't hear from us by then, call this number and ask for me. Is there anything that's worrying you most right now?"*

Don't promise outcomes, make clinical statements or give advice outside your role. If a question needs a nurse or a clinician, say so and arrange a call.

## What do you say to a referrer who is upset?

*"You're right to be frustrated, and I'm sorry that call took too long. Here's what I can see on our side: the referral came in at 2:10 and our first call to the family was at 4:30. I'm going to look at why. Can I call you tomorrow with what I find?"*

Then actually call. Specific facts and a follow-up are worth more than a smooth apology.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> An intake team tallies how 18 hard calls ended in one week.
>
> - 10 ended with a clear next step and a date.
> - 5 ended with "we'll get back to you."
> - 3 ended without any next step, and the referral was never revisited.
>
> That's 18 in total. Ten of 18 is a little more than half. The team decides that every hard call must end with a named next step, and after a few weeks they re-count to see whether the "we'll get back to you" ending shrinks.

## How do you practice without it feeling silly?

Pick one script a week and role-play it for five minutes in a team meeting. Trade roles so the coordinator hears what it sounds like on the receiving end. Then keep the scripts where people can find them quickly, on a card or a shared page, not buried in a manual.

Update them when something changes: a new staffing pattern, a new form, a difficult call that taught you something.

## How do you know the scripts are working?

Listen for fewer repeated calls, fewer surprises and a calmer tone. Check your referral records, too. If hard calls end with a next step and a date, and those dates are met, referring partners notice. It's a quiet kind of reliability.
