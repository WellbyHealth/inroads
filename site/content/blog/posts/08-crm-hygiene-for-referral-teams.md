Good CRM hygiene isn't glamorous. It's mostly small, repeated acts that keep your contact records trustworthy enough to act on. If your team has ever opened a record and wondered whether the person still works there, you know what's at stake.

## Why does CRM hygiene matter for referral teams?

Your relationships are the business. A liaison who changes jobs, a physician who prefers a certain office contact, a promise you made about weekend coverage: all of that lives in your system or it lives nowhere. When the records are wrong, you waste a visit, repeat a question or miss a chance to reach someone while they're thinking about you.

Referral work, whether at a hospital service line or a [specialty practice](/specialties#specialty-practices), has a lot of hand-offs between marketing, liaisons and intake. Everyone reads the same record, so one sloppy entry costs several people.

## Habit 1: What should be required on every record?

Fewer fields than you think. Pick the handful that you'll actually use to make decisions, such as organization, role, owner on your team, last contact date and next step. Make those required. Everything else is optional.

Long forms feel thorough and produce blanks. A short one, filled in every time, gives you something you can sort and trust.

## Habit 2: Who owns each record?

Every contact needs one named owner on your side. Not a team, a person. Shared ownership usually means no ownership, and a record with no owner is the one that goes two years without a touch.

Pair the owner with a next step and a date. If there truly isn't one, write "no action, healthy" so it's clear that was a choice.

## Habit 3: How do you stop duplicates?

Agree on naming rules and put them somewhere visible. One spelling for each facility. A format for names. A rule for what to do when a contact has two offices. Search before you add, every time.

Duplicates also come from imports and from people entering the same referral twice. Decide who merges them, and how often. A monthly merge of obvious twins takes minutes. A yearly one takes a weekend.

## Habit 4: What goes into a good note?

A note should help the next person, whether that's a colleague or you in three months. "Visited" tells them almost nothing. "Prefers a text before a call, asked for faster weekend answers, wants an update on the patient we admitted Tuesday" tells them what to do.

Keep a few lines, written the same day. If it takes more than two minutes, the habit will die. Be careful about patient details in notes. Follow your own privacy policies and your counsel's guidance on what belongs in the system.

## Habit 5: What does a weekly sweep look like?

Fifteen minutes on a set day, with a short checklist.

- Records with no owner.
- Next steps past their date.
- Contacts marked as moved or left, with nothing replacing them.
- New records that look like duplicates.
- Anything you promised in a note that hasn't happened.

Do it the same time every week and keep it small. A tiny sweep that actually occurs beats a big cleanup you keep postponing.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A made-up team samples 50 contact records from its CRM.
>
> - 38 were in good shape.
> - 7 were duplicates.
> - 3 had no owner.
> - 2 listed a job title that was out of date.
>
> The problems add up to 12 records, so 12 of 50 needed work, which is 24%, while 38 of 50, or 76%, were fine. Duplicates were the biggest single cause, 7 of the 12, so a naming rule and a monthly merge would fix more than any other step.

## What if your data is already a mess?

Don't try to fix everything. Start with the sources that matter most: your top performers and any partners who've gone quiet. Clean those, then work outward. Once the habits are in place, the rest improves by itself because new records come in clean.

It also helps to tell people why. Staff keep up with habits when they see the benefit, such as walking into a visit with an accurate record in hand.
