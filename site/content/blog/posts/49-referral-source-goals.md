Goal-setting season often produces one big number that sounds clear and tells nobody what to do on Monday. Good referral source goals break the target into the actual relationships you have, so each person knows which doors they're responsible for.

## Why set referral source goals by source?

Because sources are different. A large hospital with many discharges per week has more room to grow than a two-physician clinic. A source that already sends you most of its patients can't send much more. A source you've never worked with has no baseline at all.

A single organization-wide target hides all that. One strong source can cover for ten weak ones, or the reverse, and no one learns which relationships need attention.

## Which sources should get a goal?

Not all of them. Sort your sources into three groups:

1. Core sources: the handful that send you the most. The goal is to keep them healthy and, where it fits, deepen the relationship.
2. Growth sources: those with strong potential but small current volume. The goal is to learn what's possible.
3. Dormant sources: those that used to send and stopped. The goal is to find out why.

Everything else can sit in a "watch" list without a goal. Chasing every small source dilutes your time.

## How do you choose a realistic number?

Start with history. Look at the last four quarters for each source. Is it steady, rising, falling or erratic? A steady source may reasonably get a goal of "maintain." A rising one might get a modest increase. An erratic one needs a conversation before a number.

Then ask about capacity: staff, geography, clinical fit, and the number of patients the source actually discharges or sees who match your services. You often can't know the last one exactly, so estimate with caution and label it as an estimate. Payer mix and local rules shape what's possible too, and they differ from place to place.

Finally, ask whether the goal depends on your behavior or on factors outside your control. A target that relies on a hospital changing its policy isn't a goal you can own. A target that relies on you responding faster is.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small skilled nursing facility reviews three sources.
>
> - Hospital A sent 24 referrals last year. Goal: 24 (maintain).
> - Hospital B sent 10 last year. Goal: 15 (grow).
> - A rehab clinic sent 6 last year and has been quiet for four months. Goal: a conversation, no number yet.
>
> The numeric goals total 39 (24 + 15) against 34 last year from those two hospitals (24 + 10). That's an increase of 5, or about 15%. Almost all of it rests on Hospital B, so that's where the team puts its visits.

## What are action goals, and why pair them with numbers?

A number alone says what you want. An action goal says what you'll do. For Hospital B, the action goals might be: meet with the case management lead, bring a one-page list of what you can accept, and respond to every referral within a set time you pick together.

Action goals are within your control. Numeric goals aren't entirely. If you hit the actions and the number doesn't move, you've learned something. If you miss the actions and the number doesn't move, you've learned something different.

## How do you avoid blame and gaming?

Keep goals as shared intentions, not performance traps. A goal should help a person decide how to spend a Tuesday afternoon, and it should never feel like a verdict on them. If people are punished for a source's decline, they'll quietly stop logging referrals accurately, or chase volume with poor fit.

Never design goals that push staff to steer patients, discount, or offer anything of value for referrals. Patient choice stands, and many programs restrict this strictly, so ask your counsel if a plan comes close.

## When should you review referral source goals?

Quarterly is enough. Look at each goal and ask: is it on track, was it too easy, was it too hard, did something change? Hospitals merge, physicians retire, discharge planners move. Adjust the goal rather than defending it.
