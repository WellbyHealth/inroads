Ask ten agency leaders where their patients come from and you'll hear ten different answers, and most will be a little off. Home health referral sources vary by market, by specialty and by how long you've been around, so the useful move is to look at your own admissions rather than borrow someone else's picture.

## What are the main home health referral sources?

Most agencies draw from the same handful of places, in different proportions.

- **Hospitals.** Discharge planners and case managers send patients who need care at home after a stay. These referrals tend to be time-sensitive, often with a discharge date attached.
- **Physician and specialty practices.** Primary care and specialists order home health for patients who are declining or recovering outside a hospital. These move more slowly and depend on the office staff as much as the physician.
- **Skilled nursing and rehab facilities.** Patients stepping down to home often come through a therapy or social work team. See how the [skilled nursing](/specialties#skilled-nursing) side thinks about transitions.
- **Assisted living and other residential communities.** Staff and families look for an agency that will show up reliably.
- **Families and patients themselves.** Word of mouth, a neighbor's recommendation, an online search.

Which of these is biggest for you depends on your location, your clinical strengths and who you've built trust with.

## How does each source behave differently?

Hospital referrals are fast and urgent. The planner needs an answer today, so response time and clear communication count for the most. A physician office is the opposite: the physician orders, but the medical assistant or office manager shapes the experience, handling paperwork and callbacks. Facilities care about reliability and clear status updates. Families care about whether you sound human on the phone.

So one outreach plan won't fit all of them. A flyer might suit a physician office; it does very little for a hospital planner who wants to know you can start tomorrow.

## What does your own mix look like?

Pull your last quarter or two of admissions and group them by source type. Don't worry about perfection. A rough count is enough to start.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A made-up home health agency admitted 50 patients last quarter and grouped them by where the referral came from.
>
> - Hospital discharge teams: 22
> - Physician and specialty offices: 12
> - Skilled nursing and rehab facilities: 8
> - Assisted living communities: 5
> - Families and self-referrals: 3
>
> That adds up to 50. Hospitals account for 22 of 50, or 44%, and physician offices for 12 of 50, or 24%. The two together make up 68%, so the agency's census leans heavily on two kinds of relationship. If one hospital is behind most of those 22, the dependence is even narrower than it looks.

## Are you too dependent on one source?

This is the question most worth asking. When a large share of your admissions comes from one hospital, or from one planner inside it, you're exposed. Staff turn over. A hospital signs a preferred arrangement elsewhere. A unit reorganizes. None of that is a comment on your quality, and all of it can change your census.

Look at it two ways: by organization and by individual person. A source list that shows only facility names hides the second kind of risk. Spreading your relationships across several people at each major source is cheap insurance.

## Which sources deserve more attention?

Three groups are worth a closer look:

1. **Strong and steady.** Keep them happy. Check in, share useful updates, and ask what you could do better.
2. **Once strong, now quiet.** Find out what changed. Maybe a contact left, or your response slowed, or a competitor became more visible.
3. **Large and untouched.** Sources that send patients to others in your area and have never sent to you. Ask what they look for in a partner before you pitch anything.

Throughout, patient choice stands. Your role is to be the agency that's easy to work with and honest about what you can do, never to steer a patient. Rules about what you may offer or provide to referral partners vary by payer, program and state, so follow your compliance policies and counsel's guidance.

## How do you keep this view current?

A one-time analysis helps once. A running view helps all year. Review your mix monthly, note who's moved up or down, and write down what you did that might explain it. Over a few months, you'll notice which kinds of effort tend to be followed by more referrals, and which are only busywork.
