Good visit notes start in the parking lot at 4:40 on a Thursday, right after you leave a discharge planner's office. Without them, you're left with a vague memory that she mentioned a new unit opening, and by Monday morning that'll be gone.

Good notes aren't long. They're written for the person who'll walk in next, who might be you in six weeks or a colleague covering your territory.

## What should visit notes actually include?

Think of five small things.

**Who you saw and their role.** First name and last name, plus what they do: case manager, office manager, therapy director. Roles matter because the person who signs the order and the person who picks the agency are often different people.

**What they told you.** One or two lines in their words, such as "Says weekend discharges are the hardest," or "Mentioned the new unit opens in spring." This is the part that makes the next visit feel like a conversation and not a restart.

**What they asked of you.** A request is a gift. "Wants our evening intake number on a card." "Asked whether we take a certain kind of wound care."

**What you promised.** Write it down exactly. A promise you can't find later is a promise you break.

**The next step, with a date.** Not "follow up soon." Something like "Email the one-page sheet by Tuesday" or "Visit again the week of the 14th."

## What should you leave out?

Anything that wouldn't be comfortable if the person read it. Keep opinions about people out. Keep personal details, such as health, family trouble or gossip, out of a shared record unless your organization's policy clearly says otherwise. Nothing about a specific patient belongs in a casual note, so follow your own privacy policies and your counsel's guidance on what can be recorded and where.

A good test is this: if a stranger in your own compliance office read the note, would it look professional? If so, you're fine.

## How long should it take to write one?

Under two minutes. If it takes longer, you're probably writing a report. Many liaisons do best with a short template they fill in right after the visit, in the car, while the details are fresh.

A simple shape that works:

- Who: name, role, facility
- What I heard: one or two lines
- What they asked for: one line, or "nothing"
- What I promised: one line, or "nothing"
- Next step and date

Five lines. You can do that on a phone.

## Does it really change what happens next?

It does, because the next step is what turns a nice visit into a relationship. Here's a made-up look at what that can mean.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A liaison makes 15 visits in a month and reviews her notes at the end.
>
> - 10 visits have a note with a specific next step and date. On 8 of those, she followed up on time.
> - 5 visits have a note with no clear next step. On 1 of those, she followed up.
>
> That's 9 follow-ups out of 15 visits. Where there was a dated next step, she followed up 8 of 10 times, or 80%. Where there wasn't, 1 of 5, or 20%. Nothing about the people she visited changed. The note did.

## How do you make notes useful to a team?

Put them where everyone can find them, and in the same format. A note that lives in one person's notebook disappears when that person is out sick or changes territories.

Agree on a few shared words for the things you'll want to count later: "asked for materials," "mentioned a problem," "new contact," "staff change." They're easier to sort than free text, and they let you see patterns across many visits, like a handful of offices all raising the same weekend discharge problem.

Pair the notes with your [referral scorecard](/#scorecard) or log. If the notes say you saw a case manager on the 3rd and your log shows her referrals picked up on the 10th, that's worth knowing. If they didn't pick up, that's worth knowing too. Careful, though: the visit doesn't prove the cause. Many things move referrals, including the season, staffing at the hospital and what your own team could accept that week.

## What's the habit to start with?

Pick tomorrow's first visit. Right after, in the car, write the five lines. Set the follow-up date as a reminder on your phone, not just in the note.

Do that for a week and you'll see how much easier the next conversation feels.
