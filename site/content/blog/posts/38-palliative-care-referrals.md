Palliative care referrals tend to arrive when a clinician already trusts you and already knows what you do. That trust can't be built in the week a patient is struggling. It's built in the quiet months before, through clear explanations, easy handoffs and good follow-up on the cases you've already seen.

## Why are palliative care referrals harder to earn?

Because the referring clinician has to take a small risk. Raising palliative care with a patient can feel like opening a hard conversation, and some clinicians worry the patient will hear it as giving up. If they haven't worked with your team, they may not know what to expect.

There's also real confusion about the word. Some people think palliative care and hospice are the same thing. They aren't: palliative care focuses on comfort and support alongside treatment, and eligibility and coverage differ by payer, program and state. If a physician isn't sure what you offer, she'll likely wait.

Your job is to remove that uncertainty, one clinic or unit at a time.

## How do you explain your service in plain words?

Practice a two-sentence explanation you could say in an elevator. For example: "We see patients with serious illness to help with symptoms, planning and stress, alongside whatever treatment they're getting. We also support the family." Then add what's specific to your team: where you see patients (home, clinic, facility), how quickly you can get there, and who is on your team.

Avoid jargon the referrer doesn't use. Bring a one-page sheet, not a binder. Include your phone number, how to refer and the most common reasons a clinician might call.

If your team works alongside [hospice](/specialties#hospice) or other services, say how they differ and when a patient might move from one to the other. Clinicians appreciate seeing the path laid out.

## What makes a palliative referral easy?

Friction is the enemy. Ask yourself how many steps a busy physician needs to refer a patient to you, and then remove one.

- Accept referrals through the channel they already use, whether that's the record system, fax or a call.
- Keep the required information short, and ask for the rest yourself.
- Tell them what happens next: when you'll call the family, when you'll visit, when they'll hear back.
- Offer a simple way to ask a question before referring, such as a brief phone consult.

That last item matters. Many clinicians would refer if they could ask, "Is this patient appropriate?" without committing. A friendly answer, even when it's "not yet, but here's when to call," builds more trust than a rushed yes.

## How do you stay in touch without pressure?

Once you've seen a patient, close the loop. With the patient's permission and within your privacy policies, let the referring clinician know the visit happened and what the plan is. Follow your counsel's guidance on what you can share and how.

Between cases, keep contact light. A short note when you add a service or a team member. An invitation to a brief talk for their staff. A thank-you when a referral went well. Don't call every week asking if they have anyone for you. Patients and families choose their care, and a clinician who feels pressured will stop picking up.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A palliative team tracks one clinic over two quarters.
>
> - Quarter 1: the team visits twice, sends no case updates, and receives 3 referrals.
> - Quarter 2: the team visits once, sends an update after each case, and receives 7 referrals.
>
> Referrals rose from 3 to 7, an increase of 4, or about 133%. The data can't prove the updates caused it, because other things may have changed. But it suggests that closing the loop was a better use of time than an extra visit.

## What should you track?

Track the same basics you would for any service: referral date, source person and organization, how it arrived and the outcome. Add two palliative-specific items: whether the referral came early or late in the illness, as your team judges it, and whether the referring clinician got an update. Over time, you'll see which sources refer early and which tend to call only in a crisis. That tells you where an educational conversation might help.

Keep the notes factual and free of unnecessary patient detail.
