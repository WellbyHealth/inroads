Reading referral trends well means resisting the urge to react to every up and down. One slow week can feel like an emergency and one great week can feel like proof you've cracked it. Usually it's neither. A good habit is to ask three things before you act: is this seasonal, is this real, or is this just noise?

## What counts as one of your referral trends, and what is just noise?

A trend is a change that holds across enough time and enough referrals to be unlikely to be chance. Noise is the ordinary bounce in small numbers: a physician on vacation, a holiday weekend, one large family that chose another provider.

If you handle a few referrals a week, a swing of two or three feels dramatic but may mean nothing. If you handle hundreds, the same swing is invisible. Know your volume so you know how much wiggle is normal.

A practical rule: don't call something a trend until you've seen it for at least a few reporting periods, and until it shows up in more than one source.

## How do you tell a seasonal dip from a real drop?

Seasonality is predictable by definition, so compare like with like. Look at the same month last year, if you have it, and the months on either side. If last year's winter was busy and this year's is slow, that's more interesting than a summer lull that happens every year.

Many teams notice patterns around holidays, school calendars, and the times of year when elective procedures slow down or speed up. Those patterns depend on your service line and region, so learn your own from your own records instead of borrowing someone else's. If you only have a few months of data, say so and treat any conclusion as tentative.

## Why should you break the total into sources?

Because a total hides stories. Suppose your overall referrals look flat. That could mean everything is steady. It could also mean one hospital quietly stopped sending, offset by a new physician group that started. Both facts matter, and only one shows up in the total.

Look at the top sources individually, and group the rest. Check which sources grew, which shrank and which went silent. A planner changing jobs, a unit reorganizing or a competitor opening nearby will usually show up at the source level before it shows up in the total.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A skilled nursing facility compares two quarters of referrals from its top three sources.
>
> - Hospital A: 30 last quarter, 18 this quarter.
> - Hospital B: 20 last quarter, 21 this quarter.
> - Clinic C: 10 last quarter, 21 this quarter.
>
> The total went from 30 + 20 + 10 = 60 to 18 + 21 + 21 = 60, so it looks perfectly flat. But Hospital A fell by 12, which is 40% of its earlier volume, while Clinic C more than doubled. The flat total hides a real drop at the largest source.

## What should you do when a source's referrals really do drop?

First, check for ordinary explanations: a holiday, a change in the source's own volume, your own capacity, or a recent decline you made. Then check the people. Did the main contact leave? Did a process change?

Then reach out, and keep it human. A call asking, "How are things going on your end? Anything we could be doing better?" opens the door. Avoid accusing, and avoid assuming you know the reason. Patients and families choose their providers, and a drop can be entirely about their needs or their insurance. Rules about which providers are available vary by payer and plan.

## How do you decide when to act?

Set thresholds before the numbers surprise you. For example, you might agree as a team that you'll look into any top source whose referrals fall for three periods in a row, or any source that has been silent for longer than its usual gap. The specific rule will depend on your volume. What matters is that you wrote it down, so the team acts on the same signal and doesn't argue about it every month.

Keep a short log of what you did and what happened afterward. Over time, you'll learn which signals were worth chasing and which were noise. That learning is more valuable than any single report.
