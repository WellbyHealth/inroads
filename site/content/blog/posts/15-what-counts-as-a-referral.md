Ask three people on your team what counts as a referral and you may get three answers. The marketer says every inquiry. The intake coordinator says anything with an order attached. The director says whatever ends in an admission. They aren't wrong, exactly. They're just counting different things, which is why the monthly numbers never quite add up.

## Why does what counts as a referral matter so much?

Because every ratio you care about depends on it. Conversion rate, share of a source, response time, cost of a new admission: all of them divide by the number of referrals. Change the definition and every one of those moves, even though nothing real has changed.

It also causes quiet friction. If marketing reports a big month and intake reports a small one, someone starts to feel blamed. A shared definition lets you argue about the real question, which is what to do next.

## What are the common ways teams define a referral?

Three show up most often, and each is reasonable.

- **Any contact.** A phone call, a question, a fax, anything that signals interest. It's generous and it captures early warning signs, but it counts a lot of things that were never going to become patients.
- **A request for service.** Someone asks you to evaluate or care for a specific person. This is tighter and usually matches how intake thinks.
- **A complete referral.** The request arrives with the order or paperwork you need to act. It's clean, but it can hide the calls that came in and died because the paperwork never followed.

Requirements for what makes a referral complete vary by payer, by program and by state, so let your own intake and compliance people define that part.

## How do you pick one rule?

Don't try to find the perfect definition. Pick the one that matches how your team actually works, then make it layered. Many teams find the cleanest approach is to keep stages instead of one number:

1. **Inquiry:** someone contacted you about a person or a service.
2. **Referral:** a specific request for care, with enough information to act on.
3. **Admission:** the person started services with you.

Now everyone has a place to count. Marketing can still talk about inquiries, intake can own referrals, and the director can watch admissions. The numbers are different on purpose, and each one has a name.

## How do you handle the edge cases?

Write them down in single sentences. A physician's office calls to ask if you serve a certain area: that's an inquiry. A family member calls and asks for you by name: a request for service, even though no paperwork exists yet. The same patient referred twice by two different people: one referral, with both senders noted. A referral that arrives and gets declined: still a referral, with an outcome and a reason.

When privacy comes up, such as what details to put in a shared log, follow your own organization's policies and your counsel's guidance. Keep notes to what the work needs.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A home health agency's marketer counts every contact and reports 38 for the month. The intake team counts only requests with paperwork and reports 24.
>
> - 14 contacts were phone inquiries that never turned into paperwork.
> - 24 contacts became written referrals.
> - 16 of those 24 were admitted.
>
> The marketer's view: 16 admissions from 38 contacts, about 42%. Intake's view: 16 from 24, about 67%. Both are correct, but they answer different questions. With named stages, the team reports 38 inquiries, 24 referrals and 16 admissions, and nobody has to defend a number.

## How do you roll the rule out?

Keep it small. Put the definition in one paragraph, add the edge-case sentences, and share it in a short meeting where people can push back. Give the intake lead the job of keeping the log consistent, and revisit the rule after a month. If you keep running into the same confusing case, add a sentence for it.

Then be patient. Old habits stick, and for a while someone will still say "referral" when they mean "inquiry." That's fine. You're building a shared language, and it takes a few weeks of gentle reminders.

## What should you do with the numbers once they agree?

Look at each stage by source. Which sources send many inquiries but few referrals? Which send few but convert nearly all of them? Those patterns point to different conversations: one may need clearer information about what you serve, another may deserve more of your time.
