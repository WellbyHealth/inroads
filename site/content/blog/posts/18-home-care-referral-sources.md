Home care referral sources go well past the hospital discharge desk. Families start looking for help at kitchen tables, in doctors' waiting rooms and after a scare at the senior living community, often long before any hospital stay. Widening your view can fill the gaps when one source slows down.

## Which home care referral sources exist besides hospitals?

More people than a typical marketing plan lists. Think about everyone who talks to an older adult or a family caregiver at a moment of need:

- **Physician and clinic offices,** especially primary care and specialists who see patients with chronic conditions.
- **Senior living communities,** where residents sometimes need extra support beyond what the community provides.
- **Elder law and estate attorneys,** who meet families planning ahead or facing a crisis.
- **Geriatric care managers and social workers,** who coordinate care for families and know who's reliable.
- **Rehabilitation and skilled nursing teams,** at the point someone is heading home.
- **Faith communities, senior centers and local aging agencies,** where trust is built over years.
- **Families and past clients,** who often become your most credible voice.

Rules about what you can do with each of these, and what you can offer, vary by state and program. Check your own compliance policies and counsel before you design outreach.

## How is each source different?

Each one needs something different from you. A physician's office wants clear information about what you provide and an easy way to start service. An attorney wants to know you'll treat their client with respect and call back promptly. A senior living community wants to know you'll work alongside their staff, not around them. A care manager mostly wants to know you're dependable when they hand over a client they care about.

Visit with one question in mind: what would make this person's job easier? Then bring that, whether it's a one-page overview, a direct number for a real person, or a clear answer on service hours and areas.

## Where do most of your referrals come from?

You may not know the answer, which is a good reason to find out. If you don't record how each inquiry reached you, you can't tell whether that seminar at the senior center did anything or whether the quiet office you keep visiting is worth the time. At intake, simply ask "How did you hear about us?" and log the source and the person, if the family is willing to share it.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A home care agency logs 30 inquiries in a month and tracks which ones became new clients.
>
> - Hospital discharge teams: 6 inquiries, 3 started.
> - Senior living communities: 5 inquiries, 4 started.
> - Elder law attorneys: 3 inquiries, 2 started.
> - Families who found the agency directly: 10 inquiries, 4 started.
> - Physician offices: 4 inquiries, 1 started.
> - Community groups: 2 inquiries, 0 started.
>
> That's 14 starts from 30 inquiries. Direct family inquiries were the largest group at 10, though only 4 of them started. Senior living sent just 5, yet 4 started, which is 80%. Hospitals converted 3 of 6. The numbers are small, but they suggest the senior living relationships deserve a closer look.

## How do you build trust with sources outside the hospital?

Slowly and consistently. Show up in the same places, answer quickly, and do what you said you'd do. An attorney who refers one client and sees things go well will remember it. One who gets a missed call won't.

Share useful things that aren't about you. A short checklist families can use when choosing care, or a clear explanation of what the first visit looks like, helps people decide whether or not they choose you. Patient and family choice always stands, and your job is to be a clear, honest option.

Stay clear of anything that looks like rewarding referrals. Meals, gifts and payments to people who refer clients are heavily regulated in many settings, and some arrangements are prohibited. When in doubt, ask your counsel before you do it.

## How do you keep track of all these sources?

Make the log work for non-hospital sources. Include fields for the organization and for the individual, the channel (phone, web, walk-in, referral from a past client) and the outcome. Add a free-text reason for any lost inquiry, such as hours needed, price, or timing.

Review monthly. Look for sources that send little but convert well, sources that send a lot but rarely convert, and sources that used to send and stopped. Each of those calls for a different next step.
