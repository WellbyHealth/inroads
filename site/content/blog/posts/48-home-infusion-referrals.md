Home infusion referrals rarely arrive tidy, and the teams that handle them well ask the right questions early. Picture a patient ready to leave the hospital who still needs weeks of intravenous therapy. Someone has to confirm the order, check coverage, arrange supplies and teach the patient or a family member what to do.

## Who sends home infusion referrals?

Several groups, and they don't always talk to each other. Hospital physicians and discharge planners start many. Infectious disease and other specialty physicians write the orders. Outpatient clinics refer patients who need ongoing therapy. Skilled nursing and rehab teams sometimes refer, and so do home health agencies whose patients need an added service.

Often the person who writes the order is not the person who arranges the discharge. A pharmacist or nurse at the hospital may be the one who actually calls you. Log both the prescriber and the facility contact, because each may need something different from you.

## What do hospital teams need from you?

Clear answers to a few practical questions, as early as possible:

- Can you take this therapy, and can you start by the day they need?
- What do you need to see to accept the referral: the order, a recent lab, a diagnosis, insurance details?
- Who is the contact for questions, and how fast will they answer?
- What will the patient need at home, and who will teach them?

Coverage and authorization rules differ by payer and by state, and they change. Don't assume that what worked for one plan will work for another. Tell your referral partners plainly which payers you work with, and let them know quickly if a referral looks like it will hit a coverage barrier.

Patient choice always stands. Discharge teams are expected to offer options, and families may pick another provider. Be a dependable option, and let the decision belong to them.

## Why do home infusion referrals stall?

Typically at one of a handful of places. A missing or unclear order. An authorization still pending. A patient with no suitable home setup or caregiver. A discharge that moves a day earlier than planned. A referral that was sent to several providers and answered by the first one that called back.

None of these is a mystery, but they only show up if you track them. Log where each referral that didn't start care got stuck, using a short list of reasons.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small home infusion provider reviews 30 referrals from one quarter.
>
> - Started therapy: 18
> - Stalled at authorization: 6
> - Missing or unclear order: 3
> - Patient chose another provider: 3
>
> That's 30 in total (18 + 6 + 3 + 3). Starts were 18 of 30, or 60%. Authorization held up 6, which is twice as many as the 3 with order problems. If those six came mostly from one health system, a conversation with that system's case managers about what documents to send up front could help.

## How do you work with physician offices?

Physicians and their staff want few surprises. Tell the office what you need before you need it, and make the request once, clearly. A single page that lists the order elements you require, with a phone number for questions, saves back-and-forth.

Close the loop. When you start therapy, let the prescriber's office know within whatever your policies and privacy rules allow. When something changes, such as a missed dose or an issue at home, call promptly. Offices remember the providers that call with news, good or bad.

## What about relationships with hospital pharmacies and discharge teams?

Be present before the crunch. A short visit to a discharge unit to explain what you can do, and what you'd like to receive, tends to be more useful than any flyer. Ask where they run into trouble with providers in general. Their frustrations show you what to fix.

Avoid anything that looks like payment or perks in exchange for referrals. Rules here are strict and vary by program, so ask your counsel before planning any event or gift.

## How should you track and review?

Log the date, the prescriber, the facility contact, the therapy type, the payer category, and the outcome with a reason. Then, once a month, look at three views: which sources send the most, which ones convert to a start of therapy, and where the stalls cluster.
