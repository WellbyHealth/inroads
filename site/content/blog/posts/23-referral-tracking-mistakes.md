Most referral tracking mistakes don't look like mistakes. The report is tidy, the chart is colorful, and the numbers feel plausible. Then someone asks why a hospital that "sends the most" never seems to produce admissions, and the answer turns out to be a data problem and not a partner problem.

Here are the errors we see most often, and what to do about each.

## What are the most common referral tracking mistakes?

**Counting the same referral twice.** A discharge planner faxes the order, then sends it through a portal, then calls to make sure you got it. Three entries, one patient. Unless somebody matches them, your source looks three times busier than it is.

**Spelling the same source several ways.** "Mercy General," "Mercy Gen." and "Mercy Hospital" end up as three separate rows. Each looks small. Together they might be your biggest source.

**Logging the building and skipping the person.** If you only record the facility, you can't see that one case manager sends nearly everything. When she changes jobs, you'll see the drop but not the reason.

**Entering referrals days later.** By Friday the details are fuzzy. The "date received" quietly becomes the date somebody got around to typing it, and your response times look better than they were.

**Leaving the outcome blank.** A referral with no outcome is a referral you can't learn from. Pending forever is the same as unknown.

**Mixing up referrals and inquiries.** A phone call asking whether you accept a certain plan is an inquiry. A signed order for a specific patient is a referral. If both land in the same count, your conversion rate looks worse than it is.

**Changing the rules mid-year.** If in March you start counting something differently, the first quarter and the second quarter aren't comparable anymore, and nobody remembers why.

## How do duplicates change what you decide?

They change who you visit. A source that looks big because of duplicates gets attention it doesn't need, while a quieter, cleaner source gets ignored.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A home health agency's raw log shows 50 referrals for the month.
>
> - Hospital A: 30 entries.
> - Hospital B: 20 entries.
>
> When the intake lead matches patient names and dates, she finds 10 of Hospital A's entries are repeats of the same patients arriving by fax and then by portal. Hospital B has none.
>
> The cleaned count is 40 referrals: 20 from Hospital A and 20 from Hospital B. In the raw log, Hospital A looked like 60% of volume (30 of 50). After cleanup it's 50% (20 of 40). Same two hospitals, but the decision about where the next visit goes is very different.

## How do you fix a source list that has gone messy?

Start with a pick-list. Free-typing a facility name is how three spellings happen. Build a single list of sources, give each a standard name, and let staff choose from it. If someone needs a new source, one person adds it.

Then do a one-time cleanup. Sort the source column alphabetically and look for near-matches. Merge them. It's dull work, but it's an afternoon, and every report after that gets better.

Do the same for people. Keep a referrer record with the person's name, role and facility, so a move from one hospital to another is a change to one record and not a brand new name.

## What should count as a referral?

Write it in one sentence and put it where the intake team can see it. For example, "A referral is a request for a specific patient or client, received from a person outside our organization, in any channel." Then decide the edge cases: Does a family member's call count? What about an internal transfer between our own service lines? What about a request we can't fill because the patient lives outside our area?

The exact answers matter less than having one answer. Rules about what is allowed or required differ by payer, state and program, so keep your definition about counting and not about eligibility.

## Who should own the cleanup?

Give one person the job, usually the intake lead or a coordinator who sees every referral. A short weekly check is enough: any entry still pending after a week, any blank outcome, any new source name that looks like a duplicate, any referral with a "received" date that's later than its "first contact" date, which means somebody typed it wrong.

Then, before a report goes to leadership, read it once as a skeptic. Ask whether any number would surprise you, and if so, check that one first.

## Is it worth fixing if the numbers are only a little off?

Yes, because small errors compound. A slightly inflated source, a slightly deflated conversion rate and a few unlabeled losses can steer a whole quarter of marketing effort.

Privacy matters in this work, too. Keep patient detail to the minimum your team needs and follow your own policies and your counsel's guidance on how referral records are stored and shared.
