Every referral team lives with a gap between marketing activity vs results. Ask a liaison how the month went and you'll often hear about activity: twenty-two visits, a lunch-and-learn, a stack of thank-you notes. Ask an owner and you'll hear about admissions. Closing that gap is the most useful thing a referral team can do for its credibility.

## What's the difference between activity and results?

Activity is what your team does. Visits, calls, emails, events, flyers dropped off. It's countable, it's visible, and it feels like progress.

Results are what happened because of it, or at least alongside it: referrals received, referrals admitted, share of a source's patients who came to you, a lost partner who returned. Results are harder to attribute and slower to appear.

Neither is bad. Activity without results means you may be working hard on the wrong things. Results without activity means you may be coasting on relationships that will fade. You need to see both on one page.

## Why do teams drift toward counting activity?

Because it's easy and it protects you. If admissions drop, a long visit log shows you were trying. A liaison who is judged on number of visits will make visits, whether or not they matter.

Owners and managers fall into the same trap when they ask for what's simple to produce. If the only report that comes back is a visit count, that's what people will optimize. The fix isn't to stop counting activity. It's to ask for the next column over.

## Why is marketing activity vs results so hard to connect?

Use the source as the thread. Every referral gets logged with its facility and the person who sent it. Every outreach touch gets logged against the same facility and person. Now you can put them side by side.

For each source, you want to see: how many touches in the last quarter, what they sent before, what they sent after. Keep time in mind. Referral patterns shift slowly, so give a relationship a few months before you decide whether a visit changed anything. Patient needs, seasons and payer rules shift too, so don't credit or blame your own effort for every change.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small hospice team logs a quarter of outreach and referrals for four sources.
>
> - Source A: 10 visits, 6 referrals the quarter before and 9 this quarter.
> - Source B: 8 visits, 5 referrals the quarter before and 5 this quarter.
> - Source C: 2 visits, 4 referrals the quarter before and 3 this quarter.
> - Source D: 0 visits, 6 referrals the quarter before and 2 this quarter.
>
> The team made 20 visits in all. Referrals from these four went from 21 to 19. Source A grew by 3, which is encouraging. Source B had 8 visits and no change, so it may need a different approach. Source D got no attention and dropped by 4, the biggest loss on the page.

## What results should you report?

Keep the list short and consistent. A good set for most teams is: referrals received, referrals admitted, share of each top source's volume that comes to you (if you can estimate it responsibly), sources that grew, sources that shrank, and sources that went quiet.

Pair every one with a plain sentence. "Referrals from the two clinics we visited most went up. The hospital we haven't seen since June went down." That's honest, specific and doesn't claim cause.

Be careful with attribution. If a referral came from a hospital the week after your lunch, that doesn't prove the lunch caused it. Say "followed" rather than "produced" unless you have a reason to be sure.

## What do you do about effort that isn't paying off?

First, check that it's really not paying off. Is there a data problem, such as referrals logged under a different spelling? Is the contact the right person? Is the service you offer a match for what that source needs?

Then try a change, not just more of the same. Different person, different format, a short call instead of an office drop-in, or an invitation to see how you work. If six months of thoughtful effort still yields nothing, it's reasonable to move that time elsewhere.

Never reward or pay for referrals, and never steer patients. Marketing in this field means being a good, easy option that people can choose, with patient choice always standing. Rules for what's allowed in outreach vary by program and state, so ask your counsel if you're unsure.

## How do you start doing this tomorrow?

Pick your ten largest sources. For each, write down the number of touches last quarter and the referrals before and after. A plain spreadsheet will do. Add one sentence of interpretation each. You'll learn more from that single page than from another visit tally.
