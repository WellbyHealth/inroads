Open your team's contact record and count the CRM fields. If it's more than a screen or two, somebody has probably added a field every time a new question came up, and nobody ever removed one. The result is a record that takes too long to fill in, so people skip it, and the data you need is the data that's missing.

## Which CRM fields do referral teams actually need?

Fewer than you think. For a person at a referral source, a useful core looks like this:

- Name, role and facility, kept as separate fields.
- Phone and email, with a note on the best way to reach them.
- Relationship stage: new, building, regular or quiet. Use a pick-list.
- Last contact date and the next step with a date.
- A short note on what they care about, such as speed, weekend coverage or communication.

For the facility or practice, add the type, the address and the services it offers. That's already a lot, and it's enough to plan a month of visits.

Every field should answer a question: Who should we see this week? Who has gone quiet? What did we promise? If a field doesn't help answer something like that, it's a candidate to drop.

## Where should referral data live?

Tracking referrals themselves (what came in, when, from whom and what happened) is a related job, but a different one. Keep the referral log's date, source, person, channel, service and outcome tied to the contact records so you can see both sides. Don't try to cram patient information into a sales-style CRM. Follow your own policies and your counsel's guidance on what personal or health information belongs in each system and who can see it.

## Which fields should you drop?

Look for these usual suspects:

- **Free-text versions of things you could pick from a list.** "Type of facility" typed by hand gives you twelve spellings of "hospital."
- **Fields no one fills in.** If a field has been blank on most records for six months, nobody needs it.
- **Fields that duplicate each other.** "Last visit," "last contact" and "last activity" all saying nearly the same thing.
- **Personal details beyond the basics.** Keep notes professional and relevant. If you wouldn't be comfortable with the person reading it, don't store it.
- **Fields for one-off projects.** A field added for a single campaign two years ago tends to live forever.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A team audits its contact record and finds 30 fields.
>
> - 12 are filled in on at least 4 of every 5 records.
> - 8 are filled in on about half of records.
> - 10 are almost always blank.
>
> That accounts for all 30. The team drops the 10 blank fields, which is one third of the record, and reviews the middle 8 to decide which are truly useful and which can become pick-lists. The record shrinks from 30 fields to 20 or fewer, and the one-minute entry the liaisons were skipping becomes realistic.

## When should a field be a pick-list?

Whenever you'll count it later. Relationship stage, facility type, referral channel, contact role and outcome should all be pick-lists. That way "hospital" is always "hospital," and a report doesn't turn into a cleanup project.

Free text has its place, mostly in short notes. A line like "prefers email, ask for her before noon" is perfect free text. Just don't ask people to type things you'll want to sort.

## How do you keep the record up to date?

Make updating it easier than skipping it. A few habits help:

- Update the record right after the visit or call, while the details are fresh, and keep it to two minutes.
- Give one person ownership of the structure so fields don't multiply on their own.
- Set a small rule for when a record is stale, such as no contact in a number of months that makes sense for your pace, and review the list monthly.
- When someone moves jobs, change the role and facility on the same record instead of creating a duplicate.

## How often should you audit?

Twice a year is plenty. Pull up each field, check how often it's filled in, and ask who uses it. If nobody can name a decision it supports, retire it. Announce the change to the team in a sentence so no one is surprised.

Remember too that rules about what you can collect and share vary by program and state. If you're not sure about a field, ask your compliance lead before it goes in.
