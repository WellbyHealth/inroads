Referral sources going cold almost never make a sound. Nobody calls to say they've stopped sending patients. A Tuesday goes by, then a Friday, then a month, and one day you notice you haven't seen a particular fax number in a while. By then the habit has already moved somewhere else.

The good news is that the early signs are visible in your own records if you know where to look.

## Why do we see referral sources going cold in the first place?

Usually it's not about you. A discharge planner changes units or hospitals. A physician's practice brings in a new coordinator who has her own preferred agencies. A facility changes its process, and your name falls off the list they print. Sometimes there was a bad handoff last month that nobody mentioned.

Occasionally it is about you: a slow response, a declined referral with no explanation, a family who had a rough first week. Whatever the cause, the pattern in the data looks similar, which is why it helps to watch for the same few signs.

## Sign one: the gaps between referrals get longer

A steady source has a rhythm. Maybe they send something every week or two. When that stretches to three weeks, then five, the rhythm is breaking even if the monthly total hasn't dropped enough to alarm anyone. Look at the dates, not just the count.

## Sign two: fewer people are sending

A hospital unit that used to send from four people and now sends from one is fragile. The relationship has narrowed to a single thread, and when that person goes on vacation or changes jobs, the source disappears. Log the referring person and the facility as separate fields so you can see this.

## Sign three: the channel or the quality changes

Referrals that used to arrive by phone from a familiar voice and now arrive as cold faxes through a central queue often mean the personal connection has weakened. You may also see thinner paperwork or more missing details, which can mean the sender is no longer thinking of you first.

## Sign four: they're slower to answer you

Notice how long it takes for your texts, emails and calls to get a reply. A partner who used to answer within the day and now takes a week isn't necessarily upset. They may just be buried. But it's a signal worth writing down, along with any meetings that keep getting rescheduled.

## Sign five: a change on their side

Staff turnover, a merger, a new discharge process, a unit moved to another floor. These don't always mean a source will cool off, but they're the moments when it's most likely. If you hear about one, treat it as a reason to visit within a week or two, before anyone's habits settle.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A physician group sends referrals to a small home health agency. Here are three months before and three months after a new office coordinator started.
>
> - January to March: 6, 5 and 7 referrals, 18 in total.
> - April to June: 3, 2 and 1 referrals, 6 in total.
>
> The group went from 6 a month to 2 a month. That's a drop of 12 referrals, which is two thirds of the earlier volume. No single month looked like a crisis, but the three months together tell a clear story, and the date the coordinator changed lines up with the start of the slide.

## What should you do when you spot a cold source?

Start by checking your own side. Look at how recent referrals from that source were handled: response time, outcomes, anything declined. If something went wrong, fix it quietly and be ready to say so.

Then reach out in person if you can. Ask a real question, like how their discharge process is working lately or whether anything has changed on their team. Bring something useful, such as an updated list of the areas and services you cover, rather than a pitch. Don't mention the drop in volume or imply they owe you anything. Patients and families choose their providers, and a good partner relationship is built on being easy and reliable to work with, not on pressure.

Sometimes the answer is that nothing's wrong, and they just haven't had a fit lately. That's useful to know too.

## How often should you check for this?

Monthly is a good rhythm. Sort your sources by the date of their last referral and look at anyone who's past their usual gap. A thirty-minute review once a month beats a long annual audit, because by the time a year has passed the chance to recover the relationship is much smaller.
