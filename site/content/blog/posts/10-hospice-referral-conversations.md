Hospice referrals depend on a conversation that's hard for everyone in it. A physician has to bring up a topic that families often aren't ready to hear, and has to trust that the team they call will treat the patient and family well. If you're the person building that trust on behalf of a hospice, your job is mostly to make the physician's side easier.

## How are hospice referrals different from other referrals?

Most referral relationships are about logistics: can you take the patient, and when. Hospice adds weight. The physician is thinking about a patient they may have known for years, a family that may be frightened, and their own sense of whether a change in goals of care is right. That's a clinical and personal judgment, and it belongs to the clinicians and the family.

Your role isn't to persuade. It's to be a reliable, kind and well-informed resource when a physician decides to ask. Keep that in mind and the tone of your visits will tend to work itself out.

## How should you open the conversation?

With a question, not a pitch. Ask how the physician's practice handles these situations now, what has gone well, and what has been frustrating. Some will say they struggle to find time for the conversation. Some will say they've been let down by slow responses. Some will say they just don't know what to expect after the referral.

Each answer is something you can respond to honestly. If you can't fix it, say so.

## What do physicians and their staff usually want to know?

Questions vary, but several come up again and again.

- How quickly will someone call the family after we refer?
- Who will be the point of contact in our office, and who will be it in yours?
- Will we hear back about how the patient is doing?
- What happens if the family isn't ready?
- Can you explain what you offer in plain language I can share?

Have a short, accurate answer ready for each. Services, coverage and eligibility rules differ by program, payer and state, so describe your own organization and avoid broad claims about what hospice does or doesn't cover.

## How do you stay out of clinical decisions?

Firmly. Whether a patient is appropriate for hospice is a clinical determination made by the patient's clinicians under the rules that apply, not something a liaison weighs in on. If a physician asks a clinical question, offer to connect them with your medical director or clinical team.

Likewise, patient and family choice always stands. Families can choose among providers, and you never steer them. If a family picks another hospice, the respectful response is to help the transition go well. Rules about what can be offered to referral partners are strict and vary by program and state, so rely on your compliance team and counsel.

## What does good follow-through look like?

It matters more than what you say in the visit. When a referral arrives, respond quickly, tell the office what you plan to do, and let them know when the family has been reached. Within your privacy policies and the permissions in place, update the referring office on how things are going. Where that isn't possible, say so plainly rather than leaving them wondering.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A made-up hospice team received 12 referrals in a quarter from four physician offices and recorded what happened.
>
> - 9 patients were admitted.
> - 2 families weren't ready and chose to wait.
> - 1 patient died before the first visit.
>
> That's 9 + 2 + 1 = 12. The admission rate was 9 of 12, which is 75%. The team didn't treat the 2 families who waited as lost. They noted the date to check in gently and told the referring offices what the plan was, which is the kind of follow-through a physician remembers.

## How do you handle a source that goes quiet?

Gently, and with some humility. A physician may refer less for plenty of reasons: fewer patients who need it, a change in staff, a bad experience somewhere else, or a gap in how you've communicated. Ask rather than assume. "I wanted to check that we're being helpful. Is there anything we could do better?" is a fair opening.

A look at your records can help you spot the pattern. If you track who sends [palliative](/specialties#palliative) and hospice referrals, you can notice when a steady source slows down before it has been silent long.

## What should you remember above all?

That the person across from you is making a hard call on behalf of someone they care about. Be prompt, be honest about what you can do, keep clinical matters with clinicians and let the family choose.
