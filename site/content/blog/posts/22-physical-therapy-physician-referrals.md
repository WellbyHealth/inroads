A surgeon finishes a long clinic day, writes an order for therapy, and hands it to a medical assistant who asks the patient where they'd like to go. That moment decides a lot of physical therapy referrals, and your clinic usually isn't in the room for it. What you can control is how easy you've made it to choose you, and how steady the relationship is before that handoff.

## Why do physicians send patients to one clinic and not another?

Mostly for ordinary reasons. The front desk answered the phone. The first visit was offered within a few days. The evaluation note came back in a form the doctor could read in thirty seconds. The patient said they liked the therapist.

Notice that none of those is a sales pitch. A physician who has a bad experience, such as a patient who waited two weeks or a report that never arrived, will quietly stop writing your name. They rarely tell you. The referrals just thin out.

It also helps to remember that the patient has a say. Many patients already have a clinic in mind, or a plan network to follow, or a location that suits them. Your job isn't to steer anyone. It's to be a clear, good option when the question comes up.

## Which details should you track for each referring physician?

Keep it small, and keep it in one [source report](/#scorecard) you can sort. For every referral, log the referring physician and their office as two separate fields, the date it arrived, the date you first contacted the patient, the date of the first visit, and the outcome. Add the reason when a patient never made it in.

Rules differ by state and by payer on what therapy needs from a physician, such as a referral, a prescription or a plan of care signature, so track what applies to your own patients and don't assume. If you're not sure what your payers require, ask your billing lead.

Physician and office as separate fields matters because offices have several doctors and doctors move. If you only log "Valley Orthopedics," you can't tell whether one surgeon is sending everything and the other three have never heard of you.

## Where do referrals get lost before the first visit?

Often between the order and the first appointment. The patient gets a handout, goes home, and never calls. Or your office calls twice, reaches voicemail, and the referral ages in a queue.

Look at the gap between "referral arrived" and "first visit attended." If that gap is long for one office's patients, ask whether their paperwork arrives incomplete, whether the phone number on the order is wrong, or whether your scheduling is slow at certain times. Each has a different fix, and none of them needs a visit to the physician's office to start.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small outpatient clinic logs 60 referrals in a quarter and tracks who actually showed up for a first visit.
>
> - An orthopedic group sent 24 and 18 attended.
> - A primary care office sent 15 and 9 attended.
> - A surgeon sent 12 and 9 attended.
> - An urgent care sent 9 and 3 attended.
>
> That's 39 first visits from 60 referrals, or 65%. The orthopedic group and the surgeon both converted 75%. The primary care office converted 60%. The urgent care converted one in three, and since those patients are often seen once and told to follow up, the useful question is whether the handoff gives them a way to book before they leave.

## How do you keep physical therapy referrals steady with one office?

It looks like small, regular contact that costs the office almost nothing. A short monthly note saying how their patients are doing overall, in whatever form your privacy policies allow. A one-page sheet with your intake number, your hours and what you can see within a week. A therapist who's willing to take a two-minute call from a nurse with a quick question.

Visits are worth the time when they're short and have a purpose. Ask what the office finds annoying about the referral process, then fix one thing and tell them you did. That's far more memorable than a lunch.

Keep it clean, too. Follow your compliance team's guidance on what you can and can't offer a referral source. Nothing you do should reward a physician for sending patients, and nothing should nudge a patient away from a choice they'd otherwise make.

## How do you notice when a physician has gone quiet?

Compare each physician's last three months with the three before. You aren't looking for perfect numbers, just for the names that dropped to zero or were cut in half. Sometimes there's an innocent cause, like a physician on leave or a practice that changed its referral system. Sometimes it's your own slow response.

Make a short list each month and reach out to the two or three that matter most. A simple "we noticed we haven't seen as many patients from your office, is there anything we should be doing better?" is honest and easy to answer.

## What should you do first?

Pick one office. Look at the last quarter of their referrals, find where patients dropped out, and fix the one step you control. Then do the next office.
