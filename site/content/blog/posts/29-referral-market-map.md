A referral market map fixes a gap most teams have. Ask a new liaison to name her best sources and she'll list five without hesitating, but ask for every place within a reasonable drive that could send a patient, and she'll stall. It's a simple, honest picture of who's around you, who sends, and who has never heard of you.

## What is a referral market map?

It's a list, not necessarily a drawing, of every facility, practice and person who could plausibly refer to you, with a few columns that tell you where to spend your time. Some teams put the list on an actual map. Others use a spreadsheet. Either works, as long as it covers the whole area and not only the sources you already know.

Think of two halves: the sources you've worked with, and the sources you haven't. Most teams track the first half well and ignore the second.

## Which sources belong on a referral market map?

Start with what fits your service. A home health agency might include hospitals, skilled nursing facilities, rehab units, physician groups, clinics and assisted living communities. A hospice might add oncology and cardiology practices and long-term care. A physical therapy clinic might focus on orthopedic and primary care offices. Use your own service area, and rules for what you can accept may differ by payer and state, so include only the places you could actually serve.

For each source, record:

- The name and type of the facility or practice.
- The address, or at least the neighborhood.
- The key contacts you know, and who you still need to learn.
- Referrals sent to you in the last year, even if zero.
- The last contact date and what happened.

## How do you sort the sources?

Group them into four buckets.

1. **Regular senders.** They send steadily. Protect these.
2. **Occasional senders.** They send now and then. Find out why they don't send more.
3. **Nearby, never sent.** They fit your service but have no history with you. Most of your growth work lives here.
4. **Unknown fit.** You don't yet know if they're relevant. Research them before you spend time.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small home care agency lists 40 sources in its service area.
>
> - 5 are regular senders.
> - 10 are occasional senders.
> - 15 are nearby and have never sent.
> - 10 have an unknown fit.
>
> That totals 40. Over the past year the agency received 75 referrals: the 5 regular senders gave 60 and the 10 occasional senders gave 15. That's 60 of 75, or 80%, from just 5 sources. It also means 25 of the 40 places on the map have sent nothing. The question for the next quarter is which of the 15 nearby sources are worth an introduction.

## How do you decide where to spend your visits?

Match your time to each bucket. Regular senders get steady, brief contact and quick problem solving. Occasional senders get a conversation about what would make it easier to send more. Nearby sources that never sent get a thoughtful introduction, not a blanket drop-in. Unknown sources get research first.

Be honest about capacity, too. If you can't staff a new area or program, mapping it won't help. It's better to build trust with fewer sources than to promise more than you can deliver.

Whatever you do, keep patient choice front and center. You are making it easier for people to know you exist and what you do, not steering anyone's decision.

## What about the people inside each source?

The facility is the building. The referral comes from a person. Add a second layer to your map: the three or four people at each source who actually decide or send. Discharge planners, case managers, office managers, nurses and therapy directors often matter more than the head of the department.

People move. Update the contact list whenever you hear about a change, and spot-check the top sources every few months.

## How often should you rebuild it?

Twice a year is a good rhythm. Facilities open and close, practices merge, and your own capacity shifts. At each review, move sources between buckets, retire dead entries and add the new ones. Compare this version with the last one: did any occasional senders become regular? Did any regular senders go quiet?

A good map also tells you when not to act. Seeing 25 places that haven't sent is not an order to visit 25 places. Pick the two or three that best match your strengths and start there.
