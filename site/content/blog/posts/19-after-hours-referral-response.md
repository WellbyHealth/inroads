A good after hours referral response plan doesn't require a night shift. It requires a few decisions made ahead of time. Picture a discharge planner sending a referral on a Friday evening and hoping someone picks up. If the phone rings out or the fax sits in a queue until Monday, she'll call the next agency on her list, and who can blame her?

## Why does after hours referral response matter so much?

Because discharges don't keep office hours. Plenty of patients leave the hospital late in the day, on weekends, and right before holidays. Families are anxious, and the person arranging care often wants one thing: someone to say "yes, we can help, and here's what happens next."

You don't have to be able to say yes to everything. Even a quick, honest "we've got it, a nurse will call you by 9 tomorrow" gives the sender something to work with. A reply that never comes gives them nothing.

## Who should answer, and what can they decide?

Start with the person. Pick whoever carries the on-call phone or monitors the after hours line. It might be an intake coordinator on rotation, a supervisor, or a nurse. Whoever it is, make sure they know the limits of their role.

Then write down exactly what that person can and can't commit to. Can they accept a referral on the spot? Only confirm receipt? Must they call a clinical lead first? Eligibility, authorizations and staffing rules vary by payer, program and state, so your leadership and compliance team should set these limits, not the individual on call at 9 p.m.

## What should the on-call person collect?

Keep a one-page script so the call goes the same way each time. It should capture:

- Who is calling: name, facility, direct number and best callback time.
- Who the referral is for, using only the details your privacy policy allows over the phone.
- What service is being requested, and when it needs to start.
- Where the patient will be, such as home, a facility or still in the hospital.
- Anything urgent that the morning team needs to know.

Log the time the referral arrived and the time you answered. Those two timestamps tell you whether your system works. Follow your own privacy policies and counsel's guidance on what's appropriate to record and where.

## What do you say to the sender?

Be warm, brief and honest. "Thanks for sending this. I've got the details. Here's who will call you and when." Promise only what you can deliver, and then deliver it. If you can't take the referral, say so quickly, say why, and, if appropriate, let them know what you'd be able to do on a different day.

Never press anyone to choose you. Patients and families make that choice, and the discharge planner has to present options fairly.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A hospice program logs 12 referrals that arrived after hours in one month. 5 were answered within 30 minutes. The other 7 waited until the next morning.
>
> - Answered within 30 minutes: 5 referrals, 4 admitted.
> - Answered the next morning: 7 referrals, 2 admitted.
>
> That's 6 admissions from 12 referrals. The quick answers led to admission 4 times out of 5, or 80%. The slower answers led to admission 2 times out of 7, about 29%. The numbers are small and other factors play a part, but they make a good case for tightening the evening routine.

## How do you hand off to the morning team?

Do it in writing, and do it before the on-call person goes to sleep. A short note is enough: who called, what they need, what was promised, and what's still open. Better yet, use a shared log so the morning team sees it the moment they log in.

Start the next day with a quick huddle of five minutes. Go through every after hours referral, confirm who's calling whom, and close the loop with each sender. If someone was told "we'll call by 9," that call should happen by 9.

## How do you keep the on-call load fair?

Burnout is the quiet risk of any on-call plan. Rotate the duty, keep the shifts predictable, and make sure people get time off after a rough night. If the line is busy, look at whether the volume justifies a second person or a different arrangement.

Review the logs monthly. How many after hours referrals came in, how quickly did you answer, which senders use the line most? That tells you where to focus, and it gives you something concrete to talk about with your hospital partners.
