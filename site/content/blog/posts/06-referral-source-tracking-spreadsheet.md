You don't need new software to begin referral source tracking. A spreadsheet, a little discipline and about an hour of setup will get you a view most teams don't have. This guide walks through a layout that holds up, and is honest about where a sheet starts to strain.

## Why start referral source tracking in a spreadsheet?

Because the habit matters more than the tool. A team that logs every referral in a plain sheet for three months learns what it actually needs. A team that buys a system first often discovers it's tracking the wrong things, and moves the mess into a more expensive place.

A sheet is also easy to change. If you decide halfway through the quarter that you want to record the channel a referral came in on, you add a column. Nobody has to file a request.

## What tabs should the sheet have?

Three tabs cover most needs.

1. **Referrals.** One row per referral. This is the main log.
2. **Sources.** One row per referring person or facility, with the details that don't change often.
3. **Lists.** The allowed values for your pick-lists, such as outcomes, channels and loss reasons.

Keeping source details on their own tab means you type a physician's name and office once, not forty times.

## Which columns go on the referrals tab?

Keep it short enough that a busy coordinator will fill it in.

- Date received
- Referring person (pick from the Sources tab)
- Referring facility
- Channel (fax, portal, phone, other)
- Service requested
- Date of first response
- Outcome (admitted, declined by us, declined by patient, lost, pending)
- Reason, if it didn't convert

For the Sources tab, add the name, organization, role, who on your team owns the relationship, and a last-contact date. If you want ideas for what else to capture, the [platform overview](/#platform) shows the kind of fields a dedicated tool handles for you.

## How do you keep the data clean?

Messy data is what kills a sheet. Three small guardrails help a lot.

- **Use data validation.** Make outcome, channel and facility drop-down lists fed from the Lists tab, so nobody types "St Marys" three different ways.
- **Write down the rules.** A short note at the top of the sheet, such as "a phone call from an office counts as a referral once we have the patient's name," ends arguments later.
- **Name one owner.** Somebody checks the sheet weekly for blanks, pending items older than a week, and duplicate-looking names.

Referral data can include patient details. Decide with your privacy lead what belongs in a shared sheet, who can open it and where it may be stored. Follow your own policies and your counsel's guidance, and keep the minimum you need.

## What can you learn from it?

Once you have a few weeks of rows, simple summaries do the work. A pivot table can count referrals by source, by month and by outcome. Sort by the referring person and you'll see who sends the most and who has dropped off.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A made-up specialty practice logs 24 referrals sent to a partner clinic over two months and sums them by referring person.
>
> - Person A: 9 referrals
> - Person B: 7 referrals
> - Person C: 5 referrals
> - Everyone else combined: 3 referrals
>
> The total is 24. Persons A and B together account for 16 of 24, which is about 67%. Person C's 5 came entirely in the first month, so a quick check of her last-contact date is the obvious next step.

Reading the sheet that way takes ten minutes and gives you a visit list.

## When does a spreadsheet stop being enough?

You'll feel it before you can name it. The common signs:

- Two people edit at once and one overwrites the other.
- Names drift into duplicates faster than anyone can tidy them.
- The sheet shows what happened but nobody is reminded to follow up.
- Matching referrals to admissions means copying numbers from a second system by hand.
- The person who built it goes on leave and nobody else understands the formulas.

None of that means you did it wrong. It means the sheet did its job and taught you what to ask of a better tool.

## What's a sensible next step?

Run the sheet for a full quarter. Review it monthly. Write down every time it frustrates you, because that list becomes your requirements if you ever shop for something more capable.
