Referral intake speed is one of the few things a care team controls completely, and it's often the difference between a patient who comes to you and one who goes to whoever called back first. It's also easy to ignore, because the delay rarely looks like a problem from the inside.

## Why does the first hour matter so much?

Think about what's happening on the other end. A discharge planner has a patient who's medically ready to leave, a family waiting for a plan, and a bed someone else needs. She sends the referral to a few providers and then goes back to her list. The first provider who says "yes, we can start tomorrow" makes her day easier.

That doesn't mean speed beats everything. A quick yes you can't deliver is worse than a slower, honest answer. But a referral that sits unseen for half a day loses options before anyone has even read it.

## Where do referrals actually get stuck?

Almost never in the clinical review. Usually in the gaps before it.

- A fax that lands in a shared queue nobody owns.
- A portal message that only one person checks, and she's out today.
- A phone call taken by someone who wrote it on a sticky note.
- A referral that arrives at 4:45 on a Friday, when half the team is already thinking about the weekend.
- Missing information, like an insurance card or a physician signature, that nobody chases for a day.

If you walk through one real referral from arrival to first response, you'll likely find the stall within a few minutes. Try it with a recent one, and ask at each step: who had this, and did they know it was theirs?

## How do you measure referral intake speed?

Start with one number: the time between when the referral arrived and when someone first responded to the referring office. Not when the patient was admitted. Admission depends on dozens of things, including the patient's own choice, coverage and clinical readiness. The first response is the part you fully own.

Record two timestamps on every referral, arrival and first response, and look at the gap. You don't need a dashboard. A column in a sheet works. If you aren't logging referrals consistently yet, start by writing down the arrival date and the first response date for each one.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A [home health](/specialties#home-health) team reviews 20 recent referrals and notes how long each waited for a first response.
>
> - 12 got a response within the hour.
> - 5 got one later the same day.
> - 3 waited until the next business day.
>
> That's 12 of 20 within the hour, or 60%. Of the 3 that waited a day, all arrived on a Friday afternoon. The overall speed looks fine, but the weekend pattern is the real finding, and it points to a coverage gap rather than a slow team.

## How can you make the first hour faster without rushing?

Small changes, mostly about ownership.

- **Name an owner for each channel.** One person for fax, one for the portal, one for phones, with a named backup. Shared queues feel efficient and behave like nobody's job.
- **Acknowledge first, decide second.** A short "received, we're reviewing, you'll hear from us by two o'clock" tells the planner she hasn't been ignored.
- **Keep a checklist of what you need.** If the same three missing items stall every referral, ask for them up front in your intake instructions.
- **Plan for Friday.** Decide who covers late-day and weekend referrals before the day arrives.

None of this asks anyone to cut corners on screening. Clinical and eligibility review still take the time they take, and the rules for what you can accept vary by payer, program and state.

## What should you say when the answer is no?

Say it quickly and plainly. "We don't have staff for that area this week" is useful. Silence is not. A prompt no lets the planner move to the next option, and she'll remember you were straight with her. Where you can, say what would change the answer, such as a different start date or a missing authorization.

## What about patient privacy?

Referral information is sensitive. How you share, store and message it, including who may see a referral and through which tools, is a matter for your own privacy policies and your counsel's guidance. When you build faster habits, keep them inside those lines.

## Where do you go from here?

Pick one week, record arrival and first response for every referral, and look at the slowest three. You'll likely find a process problem instead of a person problem, and those are the easiest to fix.
