Someone, usually a finance lead or an executive director, will eventually ask what the liaison team brings back for the money. A plain way to answer is to measure marketing ROI with numbers you already have, and to be candid about the gaps. You don't need an analytics background for this. You need three or four honest figures.

## What does marketing ROI mean for a referral team?

ROI stands for return on investment. In a referral business, the return is usually admissions (and the revenue or margin they bring), and the investment is what you put into relationships: staff time, travel, educational events, printed materials and any software.

It differs from consumer advertising in one big way. Nobody clicks an ad and signs up. A physician's office decides to send a patient to you because of many small experiences over months. So the math is simpler than you might fear, but the attribution is looser.

## What should you count on the cost side?

Be generous with yourself here, or the result flatters you.

- Salary share of the people doing outreach.
- Travel, mileage and parking.
- Events, in-services and educational sessions.
- Printed or digital materials.
- Software and subscriptions.

Whatever you count, count the same way each period so the figures can be compared. Keep every item inside your compliance policies. Rules about what you can offer or spend on referral partners vary by payer, program and state, and you should never reward or pay for referrals themselves.

## What should you count on the return side?

Start with admissions from the sources your team worked on, then attach a value to each. Which value is up to you and your finance team: revenue per admission, margin per admission, or something else that fits how you're paid. Pick one and stay consistent.

Then be careful. Some of those admissions would have come anyway. A hospital that has always sent you patients isn't necessarily sending more because of a lunch. You can't separate that perfectly, so look at change: sources you increased effort on, compared with their own history.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A made-up home care team spends $6,000 on outreach in a quarter (staff time, mileage, an in-service and printed materials). It admits 10 patients from the sources it focused on, and finance values each admission at $2,400.
>
> - Cost: $6,000
> - Admissions: 10
> - Value per admission: $2,400
>
> The value is 10 times $2,400, which is $24,000. Subtract the $6,000 cost and you get $18,000, and $18,000 divided by $6,000 is 3, or a 300% return. Cost per admission is $6,000 divided by 10, which is $600. If 4 of those 10 would have come anyway, only 6 count: 6 times $2,400 is $14,400, minus $6,000 is $8,400, and $8,400 divided by $6,000 is 1.4, or a 140% return. That gap is why honesty about attribution matters.

## What should you look at beyond one number?

A single ROI figure can hide a lot. These views make it more useful.

1. **By source.** Which relationships return the most for the effort they take?
2. **Over time.** Referral work lags. Compare quarters, not weeks.
3. **Cost per admission.** Easy to explain and hard to argue with.
4. **Conversion.** If many referrals arrive but few turn into admissions, the issue may be intake, not outreach.

## Where can the numbers mislead you?

Several places. Small samples bounce around, so three admissions in a month proves little. Seasonal swings in census can look like success or failure. A big source might have changed for reasons unrelated to you, like a new hospital policy. And some of your best work, such as being dependable on a hard case, shows up months later and is hard to credit.

Use ROI as a way to start conversations, not to end them. "This source returns a lot for little effort, do we need to protect it?" and "This one takes a lot and returns little, why?" are better outcomes than a single triumphant number.

## What's a sensible habit?

Once a quarter, put the cost and return side by side by source, write two sentences about what surprised you, and decide one change. Keep the method the same so you can see the trend.
