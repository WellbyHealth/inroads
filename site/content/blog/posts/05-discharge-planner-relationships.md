Discharge planner relationships don't run on lunches and branded pens. They run on a simpler question the planner is silently asking every time she sends a referral: will this one make my afternoon easier or harder? If the answer is usually "easier," you'll hear from her again.

## What makes discharge planner relationships work?

Picture a Thursday. A planner has eleven patients on her list, two of them leaving today, a physician who hasn't signed an order, and a family member in the hallway asking questions. She isn't looking for a new friend. She needs things to move.

So what helps her is practical:

- A quick answer on whether you can accept the patient.
- A clear start date she can pass along to the family.
- No surprises, like a call at five o'clock saying you can't staff after all.
- A short update after the first visit, so she knows the handoff worked.

Notice that none of these require a visit to her unit. They require your internal process to be good.

## How do you earn trust before you ask for anything?

Reliability is the currency. A planner remembers the agency that said it would start on Saturday and did, and also the one that said yes and then didn't show. Both stories travel through a hospital faster than you'd expect.

Some habits that build it:

1. **Say no early and clearly.** If you can't take a case, say so quickly, with a reason. She can use that information.
2. **Under-promise on timing.** A realistic date you meet beats an optimistic one you miss.
3. **Close the loop.** Tell her how the patient she sent is doing, whether you provide [home health](/specialties#home-health) or another service, within whatever your privacy policies allow.
4. **Fix mistakes out loud.** If something went wrong, own it and say what you changed.

## How should you approach a planner you haven't met?

Ask before you pitch. A short, honest note works: who you are, what you do, and a question about how she prefers to receive information. Some planners want a fax with a cover sheet. Others live in a portal or want a text-style message to a direct line. Many hospitals also have rules about who may visit units and when, so check the facility's policy for vendors and outside providers and follow it.

When you do get a few minutes, spend most of them listening. What slows her down? Which patients are hardest to place? Which agencies does she find frustrating, and why? You'll learn more from those answers than from any brochure.

## What does patient choice mean for these relationships?

It means the patient and family decide. Planners generally present options, and patients pick. Your job isn't to win a planner's favor so she steers people to you; it's to be a provider she can confidently list among the options because she knows what to expect. Never ask a planner to bypass a patient's choice, and don't offer anything of value in return for referrals. Rules here vary by payer, program and state, and your compliance team and counsel are the ones to ask.

## How do you keep track of the relationship?

Write it down, because memory fails. For each planner, a few lines is plenty: the facility and unit, how they prefer to be contacted, what you promised last time, and when you last heard from them. Add a note when someone changes roles. If your contact moves to another hospital, you now know two people in two places.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small hospice team keeps notes on 8 planners at one hospital. Over a quarter, 5 of them sent at least one referral and 3 sent none. The team finds that 2 of the 3 silent planners prefer phone calls, while the team has only sent them emails.
>
> - 8 planners tracked
> - 5 sent referrals, 3 did not
> - 2 of the 3 silent planners had only received emails
>
> So 5 of 8 were active, which is 62.5%, and 2 of the 3 silent ones had a contact mismatch that was simple to fix. The notes turned a vague worry into one specific change.

## What should you avoid?

A few things wear down trust quickly: chasing a planner repeatedly after a no, leaving materials where they aren't wanted, asking about a patient's status in ways your privacy policies don't permit, and talking down other providers. Keep the tone professional and the contact respectful of her time.

## What does a good month look like?

Probably quieter than you'd think. A handful of short, useful touches: a reply sent within the hour, an update after a first visit, a thank-you note for a tough placement. Those small moments add up over time to being the agency a planner mentions without having to think.
