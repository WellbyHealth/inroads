Without a community liaison routine, the car becomes your office and the last phone call becomes your schedule. By four o'clock you've driven a long way and talked to very few of the people who actually send you patients. A simple routine fixes that without turning your day into a rigid checklist.

## What should a community liaison routine include?

Think of the day in three parts: a short plan before you leave, the visits themselves, and a close-out at the end. Each part is small. The point is that none of them gets skipped when the day gets busy, because the busy days are exactly when the field work slips.

You also need room to bend. A discharge planner who's free for ten minutes at 10:15 is worth rearranging your morning for. A routine should protect your priorities, not make you say no to a good opening.

## How do you plan the day before you leave the office?

Do the planning the evening before, or first thing at your desk, never in the parking lot. Open your referral log and pick the people you most need to see. Usually that's a mix of three kinds: a strong source you want to keep warm, a source that used to send and has gone quiet, and one new contact you're trying to meet.

Then check the practical stuff. Does the facility have visiting rules for outside staff? Is the person you want in on Tuesdays? A quick call or message saves a wasted drive. Group your stops by area so you're not crossing town twice, and put the time-sensitive one first.

Write down one purpose for each stop. "Drop off updated service area list" is a purpose. "Say hi" isn't, and it tends to produce a visit nobody remembers.

## How many visits should fit in a day in the field?

Fewer than you think. A visit that's rushed through in four minutes in a hallway rarely builds anything. Many liaisons find that four to six planned stops is realistic once you count parking, check-in desks and the unavoidable wait. If your territory is spread out, plan fewer. Honest numbers beat an ambitious schedule that falls apart by noon.

Leave one open slot, too. Something will come up: an urgent question from a case manager, a facility with an unexpected opening, a lunch that runs long.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A hospice liaison plans 6 stops on a Wednesday. One office cancels that morning, so 5 visits happen.
>
> - 3 of the 5 visits turn into a real conversation with the person they came to see.
> - 2 of the 5 end with materials left at the front desk.
> - 3 follow-up messages go out before 5 p.m., one for each conversation.
>
> The day reached 5 of 6 planned stops, and 3 of those 5 were real conversations. The materials-only visits are worth a look: if they keep happening at the same two offices, try calling ahead or a different time of day.

## What should you do between visits?

The gaps are where good routines pay off. Sitting in the car for ten minutes between stops is the best time to write down what just happened: who you spoke with, what they asked for, what you promised. Do it right then. By evening the details blur together, and a promise you forgot to write down is a promise you probably broke.

Send the follow-up while it's fresh, too. A two-line message, "Thanks for your time, here's the service area map we talked about," lands better the same afternoon than three days later.

If a conversation touches patient information, keep it general and follow your own organization's privacy policies and your counsel's guidance on what you can share and where.

## How should you close out the day?

Give yourself fifteen minutes before you leave the field or the office. Update the referral log with any new contacts and any changes you noticed, like a planner who's moving to another unit. Mark the follow-ups still owed. Then set tomorrow's first stop.

This is also where you decide what to tell your team. If a facility mentioned a new discharge process, intake needs to hear that tonight, not when a referral comes in wrong next week.

## What belongs in the weekly rhythm?

Once a week, look back across the days. Which sources did you see twice? Which ones haven't you seen in a month? Where did conversations turn into referrals, and where did visits just pile up? That review decides next week's stops more reliably than your memory does.

Pair it with your intake lead for fifteen minutes. They see what actually arrived and how it went, and you see what was said at the door. Both halves matter.
