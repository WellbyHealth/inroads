Most assisted living referrals don't arrive as a tidy form. They arrive as a worried phone call from a daughter at 4:30 on a Thursday, or a social worker asking whether you can take a gentleman who is going home from the hospital on Saturday. Both are real referrals, and they need different handling.

## Who actually sends assisted living referrals?

In many communities the sources fall into a few groups. Hospital and rehab discharge planners, who need a safe landing place quickly. Physician offices and geriatric practices, who notice that a patient can't manage alone anymore. Home care agencies, whose caregivers see the strain firsthand. And families, who often find you online or through a friend and then bring their doctor into the conversation.

Each group wants something different. A discharge planner wants a fast, honest answer about whether you can meet the person's needs. A family wants to feel that someone will treat their mother kindly. If you run the same pitch for both, you'll probably bore one and rush the other.

## What do hospital teams need from you?

Speed and clarity, mostly. A planner with a Friday discharge doesn't need a brochure. They need to know your care level limits, whether you can handle a particular mobility or memory situation, and what you'll need from them to complete an assessment. Rules on who can be admitted and what level of care a community may provide vary by state, so be precise about what your license and your own policies allow, and don't promise what you can't deliver.

A short one-page summary of what you can and can't support, kept current, saves everyone time. So does a named person who answers the phone. When you have no opening, say so plainly and, if it's appropriate, suggest they ask the family about waitlist options. A fast "no, not this time" is remembered kindly. A slow maybe isn't.

Patient and family choice always stands. Planners usually present several options, and your job is to be a good option, not to steer anyone.

## How should you handle families at the tour stage?

Families are usually making one of the hardest decisions of their year. Hurry them and they'll leave. Listen first. Ask what a good day looks like for their parent, and what worries them most.

Follow up the same day with a short note that refers to something they actually said. If they mentioned that Dad loves his garden, mention the courtyard. People can tell the difference between a template and a person.

Be careful with anything that touches a resident's health information when you talk to referral partners. Follow your own privacy policies and your counsel's guidance on what you share and with whom.

## Which assisted living referrals deserve a closer look?

Counting inquiries alone is misleading. A source that sends many inquiries and few move-ins may be sending people who aren't a fit for your care level. A small source that sends a few well-matched residents may be worth far more.

So log the source on every inquiry, then the tour, then the outcome: moved in, chose elsewhere, not a fit, or still deciding. Keep the reason in a few words. Over a quarter, you'll see which relationships turn into residents.

> **Illustrative example**
>
> A small assisted living community tracks one quarter of inquiries.
>
> - Hospital and rehab teams: 20 inquiries, 6 tours, 3 move-ins.
> - Families who found the community on their own: 30 inquiries, 12 tours, 4 move-ins.
> - Physician offices: 10 inquiries, 2 tours, 1 move-in.
>
> That's 60 inquiries, 20 tours and 8 move-ins. Hospital and rehab teams sent a third of the inquiries (20 of 60) and 3 of the 8 move-ins. Their inquiries converted at 15%, a little better than the families' 13% (4 of 30). The physician offices sent few, but 1 in 10 moved in, so a visit there could be worth the time.

## How do you keep partners warm between admissions?

Most of the work happens between move-ins. Send a short update when something relevant changes, such as a new care capability or an open apartment. Invite a discharge planner to see the building, because someone who has walked the halls recommends you with confidence.

Tell partners how a resident is doing only within what your policies and the resident's consent allow. A thank-you note that says "she settled in well this week" is lovely, but check first that it fits your privacy rules.

Notice who has gone quiet. A planner who sent three inquiries last spring and none since may have changed jobs, or may have had a bad experience you never heard about. A friendly call is better than guessing.

## What should you review each month?

Keep it to four questions. Which sources sent inquiries? Which of those turned into tours? Which tours turned into move-ins? Who used to send and stopped? Write down one action per answer and assign it to a person.
